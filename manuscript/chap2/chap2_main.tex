%See intro of Khvorostyanov about correspondence principle.

In the 1910s, the Danish physicist Niels Bohr (1885-1962)\----known for the first application of Plank's quantum theory to atomic structure models\---- formulated the \textit{correspondence principle}.
Rapidly became a fundamental principle of physics, the correspondence principle is nowadays at the heart of many theoretical disciplines.
It states that any new parametrisation, or model, dedicated to describe an observational phenomenon, should generalise the previous working theory and do not reject it. The old theory would then become a particular case of the new one.
In their immensely complete book about cloud physics \citep{curry_thermodynamics_2014}, the atmosphericists Khvorostyanov and Curry explain:
\begin{quote}
    The new theory contains a new parameter absent in the previous theory; when its value tends to some limiting value, the new theory transforms into the old theory. [...] A newer theory should be able to express the empirical parameters of the previous theories or parametrisations via physical constants. 
\end{quote}

The correspondence principle acts as powerful tool for understanding the world in a systematic way. When science strives to provide a mathematical\----that is, formal\----description of natural phenomena, Bohr’s principle offers the framework for developing sound theories that serve science’s ultimate goal: understanding.
\vspace{\baselineskip}

As indicated by its title, this thesis is the achievement of three years of modelling work. Modelling within the meaning of testing and developing descriptive theories of the world as we observe it, but also of sandbox playing in order to understand why is the world the way we see it.

Finally, the observational material used during this thesis will be briefly presented. Since I have not participated to any Arctic measurement campaign during my thesis, I will not dwell on a topic where my knowledge is only theoretical, and lacks of experience.

\section{Models and related tools}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%see the ad hoc chapter of Louis' thesis
In this section, I describe the numerical models that have been the main tools of my research work during this thesis.
They split in two categories: Regional Climate Models (RCMs) and Lagrangian particle dispersion model (LPDM). 

RCMs differ from Global Climate Models (GCMs) by the spatial and temporal scales of their simulations. As the name suggests, a GCM aims to model the climate of a whole planet\footnote{Not necessarily the Earth; GCMs are used to model other planets.}. A great example of GCMs use is the Climate Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP), from which are issued the IPCC climate projections (c.f. \cref{sec:expected_evolution}). In CMIP, GCMs from all over the world run the same simulation setup (domain, resolution, start and end date, radiative forcing scenarios) that extends over multiple decades, or even centuries. The outputs are then compared in order to get uncertainty ranges of the modelled climate evolution which resulted from the simulations.
GCMs can be coupled atmospheric, oceanic and land numerical models, in this case they are called Earth System Models (ESM).
RCMs are rather used on shorter timescales (typically days to months), and cover parts of the globe, from several kilometres to thousands. Unlike the GCMs, RCMs can run on far finer grid resolutions. While a high GCM horizontal resolution is \SI{100}{km}, RCMs can go down to \SI{1}{km}. This enables them to better represent small scale processes such as clouds, precipitation, extreme weather, or the climate of coastal regions, mountains, and urban areas

A subtype of GCMs and RCMs is the Chemistry Transport Model (CTM) class. CTMs focus on the simulation of transport, chemical transformation, and removal (by deposition or scavenging) of atmospheric trace gases and aerosols. They often run offline, that is taking meteorological fields from another model without interacting with it, like the mechanisms of the Model for OZone And Related Tracers (MOZART), or CHIMERE.
They differ from others models that are coupled with meteorology, like the Goddard Earth Observing System with chemistry (GEOS-Chem), the Community Atmosphere Model with Chemistry (CAM-chem), or the Weather Research and Forecasting model including chemistry (WRF-Chem, see \cref{sec:wrfchem}).
The latter are online models, which means they exchange informations between the weather and chemistry modules.

LPDMs are stochastic tools for the Lagrangian modelling of the transport of a large amounts of air tracers. They can sometimes be referred as CTMs if they are used to study the regional or global transport and evolution of aerosols or chemically reactive gases.

In this section, will be described the setup of a RCM (WRF, \cref{sec:wrf}), of a coupled RCM-CTM (WRF-Chem, \cref{sec:wrfchem}), and of a LPDM (FLEXPART-WRF, \cref{sec:flexpart}), that have been used in this thesis.

    \subsection{The Weather Research and Forecasting model}
    \label{sec:wrf}
    The Weather Research and Forecasting model (WRF) is a numerical mesoscale atmospheric model \citep{skamarock_description_2022}. Its development began in the 1990s and has since brought together a large international community. The original design objectives of WRF followed two main directions: short-term weather prediction through data assimilation, and regional climate simulation for research purposes. For more than twenty years, WRF has been the research tool of several tens of thousands of scientists worldwide and has become the primary framework for testing and developing parametrisations of numerous physico-chemical processes that can be used in climate models.

    % setup of wrf
    In this thesis, we mostly used the version of WRF including atmospheric chemistry and aerosols, WRF-Chem (\cref{sec:wrfchem}). WRF (without chemistry) was used to provide the meteorological fields required to run FLEXPART-WRF, the LPDM presented in \cref{sec:flexpart}. 
    WRF is a modular model, with several options available for representing atmospheric processes such as cloud microphysics, boundary layer and mixing, continental surfaces, convective clouds. In this work, I chose the WRF setup options listed in Table~\ref{tab:wrf_setup} based on past experience and best practices developed at LATMOS.     In short, the boundary layer, surface layer, and vertical mixing in the model is handled by the Mellor–Yamada Nakanishi Niino Level 2.5 Scheme  \citep{nakanishi_development_2009}. The land surface model is the Noah Land Surface Model \citep{tewari_implementation_2004}, and also calculates surface snow physics, and the energy and mass budgets at the sea ice- and ocean-atmosphere interfaces.  Grid-scale clouds and precipitation is represented by the Morrison 2-moment scheme for cloud water, cloud ice, rain, snow and graupel \citep{morrison_impact_2009}. The version of Morrison 2-moment used in WRF does not compute aerosol-cloud interactions explicitly, but implicitly assumes a globally average distribution of CCN and INP in its representation of cloud droplet and cloud ice formation processes that is probably not representative of conditions in the Arctic. Subgrid convective clouds are predicted by the Grell-3 cumulus parametrisation \citep{grell_generalized_2002}. 
    Radiative transfer for solar (SW) and terrestrial (LW) radiation is calculated by the Rapid Radiative Transfer Model for GCMs \citep{iacono_radiative_2008}, and takes into accound radiation interactions with cloud water, cloud ice, and precipitation. The 3D fractional cloud cover used in RRTMG is calculated from the output of the Microphysics and Cumulus models with the Xu and Randall XXref parametrisation. %XXLM citation https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0469(1996)053%3C3084:ASCPFU%3E2.0.CO;2
    
    Initial and boundary conditions for the atmosphere, soil and sea surface temperatures, soil moisture, snow, and sea ice are from the NCEP FNL (Final Analysis) global atmospheric analysis. In the free troposphere, WRF meteorology is also spectrally nudged to FNL winds and temperature over the spatial scale of \SI{700}{km}, to prevent the large scale meteorology in the model from diverging away from the observed regimes.

    \subsection{WRF including chemistry: WRF-Chem}
    \label{sec:wrfchem}
    Among the models derived from WRF is WRF-Chem \citep[WRF including chemistry;][]{grell_fully_2005}, part of whose development continues at LATMOS\footnote{Version publicly available at \url{https://github.com/Regional-Modeling-LATMOS-IGE/WRF-Chem-Polar}.} (see below). Originally designed for the study of air quality and atmospheric pollutants, WRF-Chem is particularly well suited for investigating aerosol populations.
    The Weather Research and Forecasting model including chemistry (WRF-Chem), is a CTM coupled with WRF \citep{grell_fully_2005}. It is fully integrated within the WRF code, and shares the same dynamics and physics with WRF.
    It can be seen as an extended version of WRF that includes all processes relevant for trace gases and aerosols, such as gas-phase chemistry, aerosols chemistry and physics, emissions, deposition. It shares the same modular architecure than WRF, and a great variety of schemes of different complexities can be used to represent each aerosol and trace gas process. Thus, complexity can be controlled in order to put the emphasis on specific chemical or physical aspects, making the model as light as possible for the intended use.
    Most of the processes represented in WRF-Chem are coupled, or ``online'', that is to say they interact with each other during the simulation. For example, modeled aerosols influence cloud formation and interact with radiation, modelled winds drive sea spray emissions, and modeled precipitation drive wet removal of aerosols and trace gases. %XXLMIn the following, we will say explicitly if a chemical tracer is physically passive.
    This property makes it ideal to study aerosol processes such as aerosol-cloud interactions.

    In this thesis, several versions of WRF-Chem have been used, from v4.3 to v4.6. The base setup of the model used in this thesis is described in \cref{tab:wrf-chem_setup}. When a different setup is used in later Chapters, differences will be mentioned explicitly.

    Physics options were kept as close as possible to the WRF setup presented above, with the important difference of the grid-scale cloud model of \citet{thompson_study_2014}, replacing the Morrison two-moment model. This change was made because the \citet{thompson_study_2014},  while very similar to the Morrison scheme in its structure and capabilities, is more flexible than Morrison for implementing new representations of aerosol-cloud interactions \citep{marelle_aerosol_2025}.

    WRF-Chem-specific chemistry and aerosol options are also described in \cref{tab:wrf-chem_setup}. In short, the gas-phase chemistry mechanism is the MOZART model \citep{emmons_description_2010}. %Xref %XXLMhttps://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/3/43/2010/
    The main sources of INP at the global scale are thought to be mineral dust and primary organic aerosols. Since these species are usually represented as chemically inert in models, and to save computing resources, we used a model setup without explicit gas-phase chemistry or trace gases. Aerosols are represented by the simple GOCART model, which includes mineral dust (5 size bins), sea spray (4 size bins), organic and black carbon (2 tracers each, hydrophilic and hydrophobic), and ammonium sulfate \citep{chin_atmospheric_2000}. For this work, I also added new aerosol tracers to GOCART for primary biological aerosol particles thought to be important for INP, fungal spores and bacteria (See section XXLM).
    Emissions of mineral dust are calculated online following \citet{ginoux_long-term_2004}, and emissions of sea spray following \citet{ioannidis_modelling_2023}. %XXLMref https://doi.org/10.5194/acp-23-5641-2023.
    New emissions of fungal spores and bacteria are also calculated online as in citet{chatziparaschos_assessing_2024}. Specifically, emissions of fungal spores are calculated as a function of temperature and leaf area index \citep{hummel_regional-scale_2015} and bacteria as a constant flux depending only on land surface type as in \citet{burrows_bacteria_2009}.
    Anthropogenic and biomass burning aerosols are emitted based on the offline inventories of ECLIPSEv6 \citep{klimont_global_2024} and FINNv1.5 \citep{wiedinmyer_global_2014} respectively, but fire emissions injection heights are calculate online according to \citet{freitas_including_2007}.
    Dry and wet deposition are not included in the base version of the GOCART model within WRF-Chem, but were included recently in the version developed at LATMOS (\url{https://github.com/Regional-Modeling-LATMOS-IGE/WRF-Chem-Polar}) following \citet{emmerson_revisiting_2020} for dry deposition and \citet{lua_further_2020} for wet removal.
 
    The versions of WRF-Chem used in this work has been used in many recent studies to investigate aerosols and trace gases in the high latitudes, from the role of aerosols particles on clouds \citep{marelle_aerosol_2025}, to the contribution of Arctic open leads to marine aerosol emissions \citep{lapere_modeling_2024}, including the modelling of complex chemical atmospheric cycles over sea ice \citep{ahmed_modelling_2023}.

    \subsection{Backward modelling}
    \label{sec:flexpart}
    In this thesis, and especially in Chap.~\ref{chap3}, backward Lagrangian modelling is used to investigate the origins of atmospheric species.
    Backward modelling consists in reversing the arrow of time of a numerical simulation. In atmospheric or oceanic models, it allows to follow the pathways of fluid parcel. When fluid parcels are flagged as being of special interest (e.g. contained particles, helped to form clouds, transported warm humid air), backward modelling helps to get informations on their origins.

    Several models are dedicated to backward modelling, and they split in two different approaches. First, the Lagrangian trajectory models which produce back-trajectories of air tracers from wind fields of a source weather model. We can cite the HYbrid Single-Particle Lagrangian Integrated Trajectory (HYSPLIT) model \citep{stein_noaas_2015} and LAGRANTO \citep{sprenger_lagranto_2015}.
    Another type of Lagrangian models are Lagrangian Particle Dispersion Models, or LPDMs. They compute back-trajectories for thousand of particles by tracking them in space and time. LPDMs also take into account the effects of dispersion and diffusion (turbulence and stochastic processes), unlike single trajectory models.
    More details on these approaches are given in \cref{P1sec:intro}.

    In this thesis, I used the LPDM FLEXPART \citep[FLEXible PARTicle dispersion model,][]{pisso_lagrangian_2019}, and more precisely its FLEXPART-WRF version \citep{brioude_lagrangian_2013}, where Lagrangian particle dispersion is driven by WRF meteorological outputs.
    FLEXPART emits thousand of atmospheric tracers that can be subject to ageing and removal by wet and dry deposition processes. It follows them back in time to produce {potential emission sensitivity} (PES) fields, that are expressed in seconds. PES are analogous to the residence times of the followed air tracer in each grid cell of the simulation domain. They are called ``emission sensitivities'' because multiplying PES (in s) with fluxes from emission inventories (for example in kg/m3/s) reconstructs concentrations series at the origin point of the backward trajectories (in kg/m3). 
    The information given by the PES, by taking into account dispersion, ageing, and giving information on air masses residence times over specific areas, gives more information on the origin of air masses than simple trajectories. In fact, we will show in section Section~\ref{sec:paper1} that trajectories, which are often used in the litterature, are only reliable to obtain information on the general direction of origin and transport of an observed air mass.
    Details on the computation, interpretation and use of PES outputs are given in \cref{P1sec:FLEXPART}.
    Section~\ref{sec:paper1} is dedicated to the development and assessment of a statistical method to analyze FLEXPART-WRF output to identify the emission sources of atmospheric particles. 
    In \cref{sec:INP_sources}, this methodology is used in order to identify natural sources of INPs in the Arctic, based on recent observation campaigns.

\section{Modelling cloud microphysics in the WRF model} %XXLM je pense que c'est mieux si c'est plus spécifique
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
    % let's talk about what happens in those schemes 
    In a climate or weather model, the microphysics schemes are the interface between the thermodynamics, dynamics and chemistry variables, and the cloud variables. They use the modelled thermodynamics to convert water vapour and aerosol particles into cloud particles (liquid droplets, ice crystal, rain, hail, snow, and graupel).
    They control the droplet formation and ice nucleation: droplets growth (condensation, collection, accretion), ice crystals growth (deposition, aggregation, rimming), and their removal by condensation or sublimation and precipitation. This affects the water vapour field accordingly, as well as impacting the land surface scheme with precipitations.
    Microphysics schemes also handle heating and cooling by phase transitions (latent heat), and provide hydrometeors quantity and optical properties to the radiation module. The latter happens through ice water content (IWC), liquid water content (LWC) and particles effective radius. The radiation module can thereby compute radiative transfer, deducting cooling or heating rates for every vertical layers, feeding back into the model dynamics. 
When aerosols particles from the chemistry modules are linked to the microphysics, the indirect effects of aerosols on the radiative budget get modelled.

    \subsection{Moments in microphysics schemes}
    \label{sec:moment_microphysics}
    Microphysics schemes are intended to compute hydrometeors variables such as cloud water, rain, snow, graupel, from the other meteorological variables.
    Among microphysics schemes, two categories are distinguished: single-moment and double-moment schemes. 
    This moment distinction has to do with the number and type of prognostic variables the model produces for every hydrometeor categories.

    Single-moment schemes only compute the mass mixing ratio of cloud particles, expressed in unit of mass by kilograms of air. 
    The number concentrations are thereby only prescribed or given  diagnostically from the computed mass mixing ratio. Doubling the liquid water path would double the mass of each droplet, rather than changing their size distribution.
    Such computational setup can not consider processes that rely on particle number concentrations.
    Aerosol-cloud interactions and their effects (e.g. Albrecht and Twomey effects) are completely put aside in these type of schemes.
    Nevertheless, in situations where detailed microphysics is not mandatory, one-moment schemes show their advantages of low computational cost and numerical stability.

    Double-moment schemes have, in addition to the information on mass, the prognostic variables of number concentration. In other words each hydrometeors are characterised by their \textit{mass} and \textit{number} concentrations.
    It allows to get dynamic evolution of droplet size distribution, addressing the associated effects on cloud lifetime and radiative properties. 
    These schemes are physically more faithful to what we know of cloud microphysics, and happen to be mandatory for the modelling of realistic aerosol-cloud interactions.
    \vspace{\baselineskip}

    In this thesis, the cloud microphysics are represented by two different schemes: Morrison's double-moment \citep{morrison_impact_2009}, and different variants of Thompson's single-moment \citep{thompson_explicit_2004}, like Thompson 1.5-moment aerosol-aware \citep{thompson_study_2014}.
    In chapters \ref{chap3} and \ref{chap4}, Morrison two-moment is used, while \cref{chap5} presents a comparison of the different versions of Morrison and Thompson schemes; where Thompson aerosol-aware will be used to specifically study the impact of INPs on clouds.


    \subsection{Modelling of heterogeneous ice nucleation}
    \label{sec:modelling_hetero_nuc}
    The modelling of heterogeneous ice nucleation goes through parametrisations that aim to represent the freezing of supercooled droplets induced by aerosol particles acting as INPs, as well as the condensation of water vapour to ice crystal.
    These numerical schemes bridge aerosol properties (number concentration, size, composition, surface characteristics) to INP concentrations, typically as a function of activation temperature.
    Depending on the approach, they can describe different nucleation modes (immersion, deposition, condensation, contact freezing), though immersion freezing is the most widely implemented in large-scale models.

Two main categories of parametrisations are commonly used. The first consists of empirical, temperature-dependent fits to INP measurements, directly yielding the INP number concentration for specific aerosol types and temperature ranges \citep[e.g.][]{demott_predicting_2010, demott_integrating_2015, wilson_marine_2015, li_predicting_2022}. While computationally efficient, these schemes are tied to the observational datasets they were derived from and are sensitive to the underlying aerosol size distribution.

The second, more physically based approach is the ice nucleation active site density (INAS) framework \citep{fletcher_active_1969, vali_quantitative_1971}. Here, nucleation is assumed to occur at discrete active sites on a particle’s surface, with the number of active sites per unit surface area determined from laboratory measurements or field observations. This method explicitly accounts for particle surface area rather than just number concentration, making it more adaptable to varying size distributions and aerosol surface morphologies \citep[e.g.][]{niemand_particle-surface-area-based_2012, hummel_simulating_2018, ullrich_new_2017}. The standard formulation gives the number concentration of INP as:
\begin{equation}
        N_{\text{INP}} = N_{p} \left( 1 - \text{exp}(-S_{p} \, n_s)\right)
    \end{equation}
    with $N_{p}$ the number concentration of aerosols particles, $S_{p}$ their surface area, and $n_s$ is the ice nucleating surface site density.
Most INAS-based parametrisations assume time-independent nucleation, suitable for many atmospheric conditions.

A more detailed representation comes from CNT (c.f. Sect.~\ref{sec:ice_nucleation}). CNT-based schemes incorporate thermodynamic parameters (e.g., supersaturation, surface tension, latent heat) and particle-specific properties (e.g., contact angle, morphology). While potentially more general, CNT implementations are computationally more demanding and require parameters that are often challenging to constrain \citep[e.g.][]{barahona_ice_2012, hoose_heterogeneous_2012, savre_theory-based_2015}.

In practice, large-scale models tend to adopt either empirical temperature-fit or INAS-based parametrisations for specific aerosol types (e.g., mineral dust, primary biological aerosol particles), while CNT-based approaches are more often applied in detailed process models or sensitivity studies.

\section{Observation data and measurements}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:observation_data}
Modelling use observations in two ways. First, the models, and especially the regional models which, by definition, do not simulate the whole planet, need boundary conditions they use as a window on what happens outside their simulation domain.
Second, models are relevant when confronted to actual observations. For instance, an atmospheric model has to be able to reproduce standard meteorological parameters as surface temperature and winds, at the location of weather stations within its domain. When it does, we call the model validated.

In aerosol-cloud modelling, modellers are interested in validating their model against aerosols particles and clouds observations. 
In the Arctic, because its continental areas are mostly remote, and the Arctic Ocean is partially covered by sea ice, this kind of observations are much more difficult to get. 
This is especially true for long and continuous time series.

    \subsection{Ice nucleating particles observations}
    INP observations are pretty rare data, although new instruments and measurement networks are setting in and developing fast. 
    An excellent example of that dynamic is the Portable Ice Nucleation Experiment (PINE) initiative, that has been developed by the University of Leeds (UK). The instrument, an automated instrument that measure INP concentrations across the full range of tropospheric temperatures, has been sent all over the world with the objective of creating a worldwide network of long time series of INP concentrations \citep{mohler_portable_2021}.

    For this thesis work, I mainly used INP measurements from marine campaigns (MOCCHA, MOSAIC, ARTofMELT). Those are presented and described when suited in the following chapters. 
    Nonetheless, I can mention the observations from \citet{wex_annual_2019}, \citet{irish_ice_2019}, \citet{schrod_long-term_2020} and \citet{hartmann_wintertime_2020} that I used when attempting to find the sources of their INP measurements. This was before I realised that those datasets do not suit reliable source tracking. The reasons for that are extensively discussed in \cref{sec:paper1}.
\vspace{\baselineskip}

        \subsubsection{Deriving INP concentrations}
        Most of these measurement are INP concentration series derived from a technique that relies on immersion freezing. It starts by a phase of particles collection, followed by an estimation of their ice nucleating abilities.

        This first step can be carried out using different techniques, each suited to its context (land-based or marine campaigns). Air filtration through micro-filters is better adapted to land-based measurements, as it requires integration over a long period of time (from several tens of hours to a few days). For icebreaker campaigns, it is necessary to use a faster air-collection technique to avoid biasing the measurements due to the ship’s motion. The most recent campaigns thereby use instruments based on particle collection on hydrophobic glass slides. The instrument directs an air intake, with a flow rate of a few litres per minute (\SI{10}{\liter\per\minute} for the MOSSI instrument described by \citet{irish_ice_2019}), through several micro-orifices onto the hydrophobic slides, where fine particles are deposited. The presence of the micro-orifices allows the collected particles to be concentrated at different points on the slide. With this type of instrument, collection times are on the order of a few tens of minutes.

    Regardless of the collection method, the techniques used to characterize the concentration of INPs in the air remain similar. Although the implementation varies from one campaign to another \citep{conen_atmospheric_2012, mason_micro-orifice_2015}, the general principle is the same. This is known as the Droplet Freezing Technique (DFT), or droplet freezing measurement. The DFT consists in immersing the collected particles into droplets of pure water deposited on a plate. The plate is then gradually cooled, and the temperature at which droplet freezing is observed is recorded. From these measurements, together with the parameters of particle collection on the filter, it is possible to infer the concentration of INPs in the sampled air.
    The expression of INP concentration depends on the technique used. Here, I present the one used by \citet{wex_annual_2019}. The number of INPs per liter of air is expressed as a Poisson statistical distribution:
    \begin{equation}
        N_{INP}(T) = - \frac{\ln (1-f_{\text{ice}}(T))}{F}
    \end{equation}

where $f_{\text{ice}}$ is the fraction of frozen droplets observed at temperature $T$, and $F$ is the total volume of air (in litres) that passed through the filter.
These data\----i.e., the air concentration of INPs active at a given temperature $T$\----are expressed in particles per liter.

The exact formula and protocol can differ from a study to another, but this illustrates the general principle of derived INP concentration measurements.
