\section{Models and related tools}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%see the ad hoc chapter of Louis' thesis
In this section are described the numerical models that have been the main tools of my research work during this thesis.
They split in two categories: Regional Climate Models (RCMs) and Lagrangian particle dispersion model (LPDM). 

RCMs differ from Global Climate Models (GCMs) by the spatial and temporal scales of their simulations. As the name suggests, a GCM aims to model the climate of a whole planet\footnote{Not necessarily the Earth; GCMs are used to model other planets.}. A great example of GCMs use is the Climate Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP), from which are issued the IPCC climate projections (c.f. Sect.~\ref{sec:expected_evolution}). GCMs from all over the world run the same simulation setup (domain, resolution, start and end date, radiative forcing scenarios) that extends over multiple decades, or even centuries. The outputs are then compared in order to get uncertainty ranges of the modelled climate evolution which resulted from the simulations.
GCMs tend to be coupled atmospheric, oceanic and land numerical models.
RCMs are rather used on shorter timescales (typically days to months), and cover parts of the globe, from several kilometres to thousands. Unlike the GCMs, RCMs can run on far better domain grid resolutions. While a high GCM resolution is \SI{100}{km} square, RCMs can go down to a few kilometres resolution.

A subtype of RCMs is the Chemistry Transport Model (CTM) class. CTMs focus on the simulation of transport, chemical transformation, and removal (by deposition or scavenging) of atmospheric trace gases and aerosols. They often run offline, that is taking meteorological fields from an other model without interacting with it, like the Model for OZone And Related Tracers (MOZART) and CHIMERE.
They differ from others models that are coupled with meteorology, like the Goddard Earth Observing System with chemistry (GEOS-Chem), the Community Atmosphere Model with Chemistry (CAM-chem), or the Weather Research and Forecasting model including chemistry (WRF-Chem, see Sect.~\ref{sec:wrfchem}).
The latter are online models, which means they exchange informations between the weather and chemistry modules.

LPDMs are stochastic tools for the modelling of large amounts of air tracers. They can sometimes be referred as CTMs that focus on the transport of particles.

In this section, will be described the setup of a RCM (Sect.~\ref{sec:wrf}), of a coupled RCM-CTM (Sect.~\ref{sec:wrfchem}), and of a LPDM (Sect.~\ref{sec:flexpart}), that have been used in this thesis.

    \subsection{The Weather Research and Forecast model}
    \label{sec:wrf}
    The Weather Research and Forecasting model (WRF) is a numerical mesoscale atmospheric simulation model \citep{skamarock_description_2008}. Its development began in the 1990s and has since brought together a large international community. The original design objectives of WRF followed two main directions: short-term weather prediction through data assimilation, and regional climate simulation for research purposes. For more than twenty years, WRF has been the research tool of several tens of thousands of scientists worldwide and has become the primary framework for developing parametrisations of numerous physico-chemical processes for climate models.

    \subsection{Backward modelling}
    In this thesis, and especially in Chap.~\ref{chap3}, backward modelling will be performed in order to investigate the origins of atmospheric species.
    Backward modelling consists in reversing the arrow of time of a numerical simulation. In atmospheric or oceanic models, it allows to follow the pathways of fluid parcel. When fluid parcels are flagged as being of special interest (e.g. contained particles, helped to form clouds, transported warm humid air), backward modelling helps to get informations on their origins.

    Several models are dedicated to backward modelling, and they split in two different approaches. First, the trajectory models which produce back-trajectories of air tracers from wind fields of a source weather model. We can cite the HYbrid Single-Particle Lagrangian Integrated Trajectory (HYSPLIT) model \citep{stein_noaas_2015} and LAGRANTO \citep{sprenger_lagranto_2015} as such models.
    On the other hand are the LPDMs. The latter compute back-trajectories for thousand of air tracers by tracking them in space and time. This allow to take into account dispersion (turbulence and stochastic processes), unlike trajectory models.
    More details on these approaches are given in Sect.~\ref{P1sec:intro}.

    In this thesis, is used the LPDM FLEXPART \citep[FLEXible PARTicle dispersion model,][]{pisso_lagrangian_2019}, and more precisely the FLEXPART-WRF version \citep{brioude_lagrangian_2013} that is configured to run on WRF meteorological outputs.
    FLEXPART emits thousand of atmospheric tracers that can be subject to removal by wet and dry deposition processes. It follows them back in time to produce {potential emission sensitivity} (PES) fields, that are expressed in seconds. Represented as plumes, PES are analogous to the residence times of the followed air tracer in each grid cell of the simulation domain, and are initially intended to be multiplied with emission inventories in order to get emission fluxes. 
    The information given by the PES surpasses the simple direction of the origins commonly given by trajectories models.
    Details on the computation, interpretation and use of PES outputs are explored in Sect.~\ref{P1sec:FLEXPART}.
    Section~\ref{sec:paper1} is dedicated to the development and assessment of an analysis methodology of FLEXPART outputs in order to identify the emission sources of atmospheric particles. 
    In Sect.~\ref{sec:INP_sources}, this methodology is used in order to identify natural sources of INPs in the Arctic, based on recent observation campaigns.
