\section{Introduction and state of the science}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:intro_chap4}

%\todo{Short intro sentence to link with the previous chapter}
 
% ice cloud in polar regions
The climate of polar regions, where ice-containing clouds are predominant \citep{matus_role_2017}, is highly sensitive to cloud ice particles.
Indeed, the latter determine the physical properties of clouds by exerting a strong influence on physical properties such as lifetime and phase partitioning, which will therefore influence precipitation and radiative transfer \citep{cantrell_production_2005}.
Whether cloud ice forms predominantly from heterogeneous nucleation, or that secondary ice production (SIP) dominates, is still a scientific debate \citep{zhao_primary_2022}.
Nevertheless, cloud ice systematically originates from a nucleation event, which can itself be of different natures.
In homogeneous nucleation, the freezing rate depends on temperature and water vapour super-saturation, and happens without the assistance of any foreign substance \citep{vali_technical_2015}. % XXJC redondant car la saturation par rapport à la glace est fixée pour T donnée.
The initial nucleation event can be followed by secondary processes that magnify the ice production \citep{ickes_classical_2015}. 
In mixed-phase clouds (MPCs), supercooled liquid water can evaporate at the expense of ice crystals through the Wegener-Bergeron-Findeisen (WBF) effect \citep{korolev_limitations_2007}.

% introduction to hetero nucleation and INPs
In the Arctic, where the temperature at cloud height do not systematically reach the homogeneous ice nucleation threshold of $-38$ \unit{\degree C}, the particles responsible for the heterogeneous ice formation are critical. These Ice Nucleating Particles (INPs), can be of many different types, but are usually of natural origin. 
%In the remote region of high Arctic, natural sources predominate over anthropogenic emissions \citep{hartmann_variation_2019}.  %XXLM  je retirerais, pas vraiment exact pour la plupart des espèces, par ex  en masse le sulfate est majoritairement anthropique. C'est vrai pour les INP mais ils sont majoritairement 100% naturels donc c'est aussi le cas hors arctique
The main types of particles that contribute to INP are mineral dust from deserts, river beds, shores and glacial valleys \citep{meinander_newly_2022}, and biological matter from the terrestrial and the marine biosphere \citep{cornwell_bioaerosols_2023}. 

% what are the important particles for INPs
The aerosol particles ability to nucleate ice highly depends on their nature, which will affect the nucleation temperature and rate \citep{murray_ice_2012, hoose_heterogeneous_2012, kanji_overview_2017}.
In particular, the size (especially surface area) and the microscopic structure of the particles are important for their nucleation ability.

Because of their large sizes, and because of their high emission rates, coarse-mode mineral dust particles are often recognised as the most important type of INP globally \citep[][]{kok_mineral_2023}. 
But in remote marine environments such as central Arctic, this predominance is questioned, and marine biogenic particles could get the upper hand over dust \citep{burrows_ice_2013}, although some research indicates that dust remain strong contributors over marine sources \citep[][]{si_concentrations_2019}.

Additionally, several types of biogenic particles are active as INP at warmer temperatures than dust.
They can be fungal spores, virus, bacteria, vegetation debris, and can originate from a broad range of environments \citep{despres_primary_2012}: marine sources \citep[][]{demott_sea_2016, creamean_ice_2019, irish_ice_2019, ickes_ice-nucleating_2020, kojoj_arctic_2024}, terrestrial vegetation \citep[][]{pereira_freitas_regionally_2023} and soil \citep[][]{creamean_thawing_2020, jensen_linking_2025}. 
Biological species that are particularly effective in ice nucleation, such as the \textit{Pseudomonas Syringae} bacteria that is among the most common species found in clouds \citep{vaitilingom_long-term_2012}, can trigger ice formation from \tempc{-3} \citep{maki_ice_1974, amato_survival_2015} and below \tempc{-24} \citep{vogel_ice-nucleating_2024}. In comparison, the K-Feldspar, most studied mineral for ice nucleation, does not efficiently nucleate at temperatures above \tempc{-12} \citep{connolly_studies_2009, ansmann_influence_2008}.
% the sources of INPs remain blurry
The contributions of the different types of aerosols to INPs remain uncertain. Many studies have tried to identify main sources of INP, but no clear conclusion emerged yet.
Furthermore, the tracing of the sources of observed INP as performed in the literature is not always reliable \citep[][]{da_silva_how_2025}.
    
% what is the problematic?
    % knowledge on INPs
    In the frame of an accelerating global warming, and especially with the Arctic suffering polar amplification \citep{serreze_processes_2011, rantanen_arctic_2022, taylor_process_2022}, the efforts to better model the climate have intensified.
    Considering the importance of ice clouds in this context, it is crucial to investigate the role of INPs  \citep{murray_opinion_2021}.
    The representation of INPs in models has even be suggested to be a key variable in the modelling of Arctic amplification \citep[][]{tan_potential_2022}.
    
    % modelling INPs
    Work on ice nucleation modelling goes back to the mid 20th century with the immersion freezing parametrisation of \citet{bigg_formation_1953}, the investigation on contact freezing by \citet{young_role_1974} and the study of INP active site density by \citet{fletcher_active_1969}, and more recently with the implementation of condensation and deposition freezing in a cloud model by \citet{meyers_new_1992}.
    In the last decades, the legacy of these studies has been accompanied by an increasing number of measurement campaigns. It brought new knowledge for species and region specific parametrisations \citep[e.g.][]{demott_predicting_2010, hoose_classical-theory-based_2010, niemand_particle-surface-area-based_2012, burrows_ice_2013, demott_integrating_2015, wilson_marine_2015, li_predicting_2022, gjelsvik_using_2025}.
    % observation perspective
    From the observation perspective, both marine and terrestrial PBAPs appear to be of greater importance for Arctic INPs. 
    \citet{kojoj_arctic_2024} demonstrate the importance of fluorescent PBAPs (fPBAPs) during peaks of INP concentrations in the central Arctic, while \citet{barry_bioaerosols_2025} show the important contribution of bioaerosols for INPs in the same region.
    % efforts of implementation in models
    In parallel, efforts are made to implement this knowledge on ice nucleation modelling within aerosol-aware atmospheric cloud models \citep[][]{gettelman_global_2010, hummel_simulating_2018, li_predicting_2022, kawai_dominant_2023, gjelsvik_using_2025}.
    The conclusions of these experiments highly vary from a study to another.
    With a dust focus, \citet{kawai_dominant_2023} emphasise the dominant role of local dust particles for Arctic INPs; results corroborated by the PBAP focused study by \citet{hummel_simulating_2018}. \citet{mccluskey_numerical_2019} have shown that modelled INPs from dust and sea sprays can accurately reproduce observation in the North eastern Atlantic.
    In parallel, \citet{chatziparaschos_assessing_2025} highlight the seasonal and regional variability of the dominant contribution to INPs between dust and PBAPs from marine and terrestrial sources.
    This discrepancy between the modelling and observational point of views shows that further work is needed to discern what type of particles drive the INP populations in the Arctic. 
    
% declaration about what we want to achieve here
In this chapter, we use modelling tools to investigate the role of high latitude primary aerosols particles for the INP Arctic populations, with a focus on the respective contributions of PBAPs and mineral dust. Relying on both observations and modelling, we try to reconcile both perspectives.
    
%    Demott 2010 says observations can not be extrapolated to some other places.
%  [terminology by vali 2015]

\vspace{\baselineskip}
% what we do in this study
The Weather Research and Forecasting model including chemistry (WRF-Chem) is used (c.f. \cref{chap2}), along with a set of ice nucleation parametrisations, in order to (1)~evaluate its ability to represent in situ INP observations, (2)~investigate the effect of in-cloud activation and the removal of efficient INP on INP concentrations, (3)~get insights on the spatial distribution of Arctic INP.
    % what is set up
    In a first offline approach (i.e. modelled INPs are calculated but do not influence the formation of ice in the cloud model, and their removal as INP is not directly influenced by the modelled physics), different ice nucleation parametrisations are applied to both modelled and observed coarse mode aerosols particles in the Central Arctic. The modelled aerosols are composed of mineral dust particles emitted by middle and high latitude sources, including Iceland, Northern Canada, and Northern Russia. Marine primary organic aerosols (MPOA) also contribute to the coarse mode modelled particles. Those are emitted along with sea spray in open ocean regions, and have a dependence with the marine biological activity.
    The observed particles are from the Microbiology-Ocean-Cloud-Coupling in the High Arctic (MOCCHA) 2018, and the Multidisciplinary Drifting Observatory for the Study of Arctic Climate (MOSAiC) 2020 \citep{frickenhaus_data_2022} Arctic campaigns. 
    The primary biological aerosol particles (PBAP) are distinguished from the total coarse-mode particles.
    The INP observations used to compare the modelling results are also part of the campaigns datasets.
    
    In a second online\---but not cloud coupled\---approach (i.e. the modelled INPs are not diagnosed but are emitted, transported and removed according to the model's physics), INP tracers are directly emitted from the aerosol sources. The tracers are defined for 5 different upper activation temperatures, binned with a 5 degrees interval and range from $-5$ \unit{\degree C} to $-25$ \unit{\degree C}.
    The INP tracers are then dynamically transported during the simulation, and are subject to dry and wet deposition, as well as removal by in-cloud activation.
    This second set-up allows for the reproduction of INP concentration time series that are comparable to those obtained from the first approach and from the observations.
   But it also enables the quantification of in-cloud removal processes, and especially the preferential removal of the most efficient INPs active at the highest temperatures, as well as the investigation of the spatial distribution of INPs and of the emission sources contributions. 
    Finally, this innovative approach opens the door to the implementation of online aerosol-cloud interactions which would benefit of the same  flexibility and low computational cost that this modelling set-up offers.

In a first section, the modelling tools are presented with a detailed description of coarse-mode aerosol emissions.
Then, the observational data from the two campaigns are described.

    \subsection{WRF-Chem modelling set-up}
    \label{sec:wrf_set-up}
    % general presentation
    The simulations and development presented in this study are performed with WRF‐Chem version 4.6.1, including optimisations for polar regions described in \citet{marelle_improvements_2017, ioannidis_modelling_2023, lapere_modeling_2024, marelle_aerosol_2025}. 
    
    % domain set-up
    The simulation domain is a polar centred square of approximately $12,000 \times 12,000$ \si{\km}, or $240 \times 240$ cells of \SI{50}{\km} resolution. 
    It is composed of 49 vertical levels, ranging from the surface up to \SI{50}{\hecto\pascal}, with the first vertical level from 0 to \SI{20}{\meter}}.
    % aerosol set-up
    The complete WRF-Chem set-up (microphysics, physics, chemistry, aerosols) is described in \cref{tab:wrf-chem_set-up}, with new additions for this study described below.

    \subsection{High latitude dust emissions}
    \label{sec:dust_emiss}
    The dust emission parametrisation in the model is based on \citet{ginoux_long-term_2004} with several important modifications to correct the shortcomings of the parametrisation, as described in \citet{legrand_afwa_2019}. 
    The threshold velocity for saltation was modified to be a function of soil grain size, rather than aerosol size, using parameters from \citet{legrand_afwa_2019}. 
    The predicted friction threshold velocity was scaled to a 10-m velocity using the ratio of these two quantities in the model, so that it could be used with the 10-m wind speed formulation in \citet{ginoux_long-term_2004}. The soil moisture dependence of the threshold velocity was modified to follow \citet{fecan_parametrization_1999}, allowing for a decrease in emissions with soil moisture.
    The emission size distribution was changed to follow \citet{kok_scaling_2011} theory and \citet{legrand_afwa_2019}, and is described in \cref{tab:dust_bins}.
    The erodibility source parameter, that represents the fraction of a model grid cell capable of emitting dust, was recalculated to include high latitude sources. This calculation follows the procedure described in \citet{groot_zwaaftink_substantial_2016}, and combines data from the Global Land Cover by National Mapping Organizations \citep[GLCNMO,][]{national_mapping_organizations_global_2025} land surface inventory, and the Earth Topography 2022 \citep[ETOPO,][]{noaa_national_centers_for_environmental_information_etopo_2022} digital elevation model.

    \subsection{Sea spray emissions}
    \label{sec:seaspray_emiss}
    The sea spray source function used in this work is from \citet{ioannidis_modelling_2023} (IO2023). It has been developed, implemented and evaluated in the WRF-Chem model specifically for Arctic studies. It is based on the sea spray source function of \citet{gong_parameterization_2003}, with enhanced emissions in the fine mode, and a new dependence on SST and wind speed based on satellite measurement of white cap fractions.
    It is thus more region specific than the largely used \citet{gong_parameterization_2003} and \citet{monahan_model_1986}, often found in global models, such as those of the Climate Model Intercomparison Panel 6 (CMIP6) \citep{lapere_representation_2023}.

    % In this study, the sea spray aerosols will be considered as the sum of the sea salt aerosols and the MPOA since are only considered coarse mode particles.

    \subsection{Continental primary biological aerosols particles}
    \label{sec:pbap_emiss}
    Primary biological aerosols particles (PBAPs) can be of various different species. In this study, we model PBAPs from ocean regions as MPOA (c.f. Sect. \ref{sec:seaspray_emiss}), and PBAPs from the continental biosphere from bacteria and fungal spores. Pollen are not considered in this study because they are assumed to be too large to be efficiently transported to the Arctic Ocean.
    The fungal spore emissions follow \citet{hummel_regional-scale_2015} parametrisation, which relies on local temperature and specific humidity conditions, together with the leaf area index (LAI). The LAI in WRF is monthly varying, and constructed from the Global Landcover 2000 Database (GLC2000).
    The emission flux density of fungal spores $F_{\text{FS}}$, expressed in \si{\per\square\metre\per\second}, is given by the following equation:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:fungal_spores}
        F_{\text{FS}} = b_1\, T+b_2\, q_v\, \text{LAI}
    \end{equation}
    where $T$ is the surface temperature in degrees Celsius, $qv$ the specific humidity in \si{kg\per kg}, and LAI the leaf area index in \si{\square\metre\per\square\metre}.
    
    The bacteria emissions is based on the parametrisation from \citet{burrows_bacteria_2009}.
    It relies on constant bacteria emission rates dependent of the surface types, derived from inversion modelling of measurements. The surface types are derived from the MODIS surface categories, mapped to the categories of \citet{burrows_bacteria_2009}. The details of the lumped ecosystems classes and the associated emission rates are given in the supplementary materials of the original paper \citep[][]{burrows_bacteria_2009}.

    \subsection{Ice nucleation modelling } 
    \label{sec:inp_param}
    Ice nucleation parametrisations convert aerosol particles concentrations to number concentrations of INP as a function of activation temperature.
    In this study, we focus only on the representation of the immersion mode of nucleation.
    This choice is motivated by the fact it is the core of the INP measurement technique used for the great majority of INP measurements (particularly by the data used in this study). Consequently, fair comparisons of model and observation encourage the use of this nucleation mode.

    % Types of nucleation schemes
    Nucleation schemes can rely on various different approaches. Some of them are temperature-dependent functions fitted directly to INP observations, that give a number concentration of INP as a function of temperature. They are therefore valid for a specific temperature range and particle type that correspond to the initial observations, and have strong dependence on the assumed particle size distribution \citep[e.g.][]{demott_predicting_2010, demott_integrating_2015, wilson_marine_2015, li_predicting_2022}.
    Others use a slightly more complex approach, based on ice nucleation active surface site (INAS) density, also derived from observations. This deterministic approach assume that freezing happens at specific sites on the particle \citep{fletcher_active_1969, vali_quantitative_1971}, active at a given temperature. The nucleation also depends on the aerosol particle surface area, rather than just on its number concentration. The INAS densities are determined with fitted experiments or measurements, and are consequently dependent on the particles properties \citep[e.g.][]{connolly_studies_2009, murray_ice_2012, niemand_particle-surface-area-based_2012, ullrich_new_2017, hummel_simulating_2018, mccluskey_marine_2018}.
    The standard equation of this approach has the following forms:
    \begin{equation}
        N_{\text{INP}} = N_{p} \left( 1 - \text{exp}(-S_{p}\, n_s)\right)
        \label{eq:Ninp_inas}
    \end{equation}
    with $N_p$ the number of aerosols particles, $S_p$ their surface area (\si{\square\meter}), and $n_s$ is the ice nucleating surface site density (\si{\per\square\meter}). The latter is where the different parametrisations apply.
    These parametrisations make the assumption that nucleation is a time-independent, deterministic process. 
    
    A contrario, the classical nucleation theory (CNT), takes a deeper step into the physics of ice crystal formation, considering ice nucleation as a stochastic process \citep{pruppacher_microphysics_1998, ickes_classical_2015}.
    More complex than the previous approaches, the CNT considers many factors, including thermodynamic parameters and particles properties \citep{keita_new_2020}. Those parameters can be difficult to determine \citep[e.g.][]{chen_parameterizing_2008, barahona_ice_2012, hoose_heterogeneous_2012, savre_theory-based_2015}, and the implementation of such an approach is computationally heavier than the previous methods.

    \subsubsection{Parameters of the INAS parametrisations}
    \label{sec:inas_parameters}
    In the following sections, the INAS based parametrisations will be used for the production of INP tracers. Here, are described in details how they are set up.

    The number of INPs is given by Eq.~\ref{eq:Ninp_inas}. The INAS term is specific to every parametrisation, but each follows an equation of the form:
    \begin{equation}
        n_s(T) = \text{exp}(\alpha\,T + \beta) 
        \label{eq:inas}
    \end{equation}
    The number of particle $N_p$ of \cref{eq:Ninp_inas} can also be modulated by a factor $\epsilon$ that represents the abondance of potential INPs among the considered particles, and by $\gamma$, the fraction of maximum activation at low temperature for the considered species.
    All together, the complete equation expresses as,
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{N_{\text{INP}}(T)}{N_p} = \epsilon \gamma \left( 1 - \text{exp}(-S_{p}\, \text{exp}(\alpha\,T + \beta) \right) 
        \label{eq:total_Ninp_inas}
    \end{equation}

    The parameters are all derived from observations, and listed in \cref{tab:inas_param}.

The exponential form of the INAS parametrisation makes it tends toward a plateau, of which the magnitude is driven by the $gamma$ parameter. 
The low $\gamma$ values of H18 parametrisations have the effect of capping the maximum activation fraction they can reach. 
Even if the activation fraction depends on both temperature and particle surface area, they cease to be effective once the plateau is reached.

Figure \ref{fig:activation_fraction} shows the evolution of the activation fraction for H18B, M18 and N12 against temperature and particle diameter.
All reach \perc{100} activation because no $\gamma$ factor is applied. The saturated zone on the left of the diagrams shows the parameter space (temperature and particle diameter) for which the parametrisations predict activation comprised within \perc{10} and \perc{100}. It happens more or less easily, depending on the nature of the parametrisation. 
Once this threshold is reached, either temperature or particle diameter affect the activation efficiency.
For H18B (panel A), the considered particles, which are bacteria, are known to be active at warm temperatures. Consequently, the parametrisation predicts high potential activation (later modulated by the $\gamma$ factor) relatively early, around \tempc{-10} for particles of \SI{1}{\um}. 
Comparatively, it happens from \tempc{-27} for the dust particles in N12, considering the same particle size.

The slope of the activation fraction step lines on the diagrams accounts for the weight of the two parameters (temperature and particle diameter). 
At a given temperature, the biggest particles are more activated; the slope is all the more steep as the particle size is given an important role by the parametrisation. 
In other words, vertical lines would indicate no effect of the size, while horizontal lines  mean no temperature dependence.
H18B is more sensitive to temperature than M18, and N12 shows the strongest dependence in particle diameter.
In term of modelling, the latter means that parametrisations sensitive to particle size will suffer more of imprecisions on the modelled particle size distributions.

    \subsection{INP emissions}
    \label{sec:method_inp_emission}
    This second approach uses the best performing ice nucleation parametrisations identified with the first approach to convert modelled dust, PBAPs (bacteria and fungal spores) and sea sprays into INPs, directly at emission.
    INP tracers are emitted from this conversion into five classes, differentiated by their activation temperature and ranging from \tempc{-5} to \tempc{-25}, with a five degrees Celsius increment.
    This method allows us to derive an INP emission flux directly, rather than an aerosol emission flux, and then to use the model to transport, mix and remove INP tracers rather than aerosol tracers.
    
    In order to convert aerosol emissions into INP tracers active at the temperature $T$, we use INAS based and species specific parametrisations set up at $T$.
    The INPs from dust particles are produced with the parametrisation by \citet{niemand_particle-surface-area-based_2012}.
    INPs from sea sprays can be produced with two different approaches. First, the organic mass fraction of sea sprays can be considered as active bacteria and converted into INPs with the \citet{hummel_simulating_2018} parametrisation. However, this assumption is unlikely since it assumes that 100\% of the organic matter in sea spray is bacteria, instead of other more common organic material including simpler semi-volatile species. In addition, the analysis of chemical species made in parallel of the \citet{kojoj_arctic_2024} study suggested that marine organic aerosols observed during MOCCHA did not contain \textit{Pseudomonas syringae} bacteria.
    Alternatively, the total sea spray can be used to produce INPs with the parametrisation from \citet{mccluskey_marine_2018}, which has shown good performances in reproducing INP concentrations when combined with dust parametrisations \citep[][]{mccluskey_numerical_2019}.
    We excluded the MPOA INP parametrisation from \citet{wilson_marine_2015} because of its poor performances demonstrated in \citet{mccluskey_numerical_2019}. %and verified with our set-up.
    Table \ref{tab:param_emiss_inp} indexes the parametrisations used for the emissions of INPs by the different aerosol species.

    \subsection{INP tracer removal and activation}
    \label{sec:removal}
    The main advantage of the second approach with INP tracers is the ability to monitor the activation of the INPs, and to reproduce the removal of efficient INPs after their activation in ice clouds.
    % INP removal
    The activation mechanism is modelled as a removal of the INP tracers. 
    In this study, post-activation resuspension of INPs is not modelled. Therefore, in the following, the terms ``activation'' and ``removal'' will be synonyms. 
    Nevertheless, this set-up is suitable for testing INP resuspension parametrisations.
    
    The temperature threshold is the main driver of the activation process. A temperature condition is thus used to remove the INPs of which the activation temperature meets the condition.
    The activation of $N_a$ INPs at the temperature $T$ (i.e. INPs configured as active at the temperature $T$), will lead to the activation of the INPs classes that correspond to temperatures higher than $T$.
    The amount $N_a$ of INP removed is the same in all the temperature classes that meet the temperature criterion.
    $N_a$ can be modulated by different meteorological conditions, such as relative humidity, liquid water content or cloud fraction. % what do we use ? 
    In the set-up used here, the removal of INPs depend only on the temperature and the model cloud fraction:
    \begin{equation}
        N_a = N_{\text{INP}} * (1-f_{\text{cloud}})
    \end{equation}
    with $N_{\text{INP}}$ the number of INPs active at the temperature $T$ and above, and $f_{\text{cloud}}$ the model cloud fraction.

        \subsubsection{Modelled aerosol concentrations}
        \label{sec:aer_emissions}
        Figure \ref{fig:coarse_concentrations} depicts the modelled particles concentrations spatially interpolated along the ship track of the MOCCHA campaign.
        Panel A shows the modelled dust mass concentrations for the five size bins.
        Larger dust particles are generally the less abundant, but are more variable in time than the smaller dust bins.
        In particular, the smallest bin (effective $d_{\text{p}} = 1.46$ \si{\um}) dominates most of the background signal, but can sometimes be overtaken by contributions from larger bins, most likely due to events of strong atmospheric transport.
        The size-resolved representation shows the effective size-sorting by transport and deposition processes (c.f. Sect. \ref{sec:wrf_set-up}).
        
        On panel B, the total PBAP (thick green line) stays mostly between \SI{0.02}{\ug\per\cubic\metre} and \SI{1}{\ug\per\cubic\metre}, with relatively smooth variations compared to dust.
        This lower variability suggests smoother emission patterns and more uniform sources.
        The MPOA concentrations mostly drives the total PBAP. Fungal spores and bacteria are hardly transported from their remote continental emission sources. This is the expected behaviour in the central Arctic, where the MOCCHA campaign took place.
        However, the continental PBAPs, especially fungal spores,  sometimes rise to higher concentrations, because of transport events, temporarily overshooting the MPOAs.
        
    
        On panel C, observed coarse-mode aerosol mass concentrations (black dashed-line) are also shown. They were calculated by integrating the coarse mode size distribution, assuming a mean aerosol density of 1500  \si{\kg\per\cubic\metre}. The coarse mode observations show high variability, with frequent peaks above 1 \si{\ug\per\cubic\metre} up to vales of more than 10  \si{\ug\per\cubic\metre}.
        The total modelled-derived concentration underestimates the highest values  at the beginning and the end of the campaign, but reproduces the average concentrations and the variability rather well between 15 August and 10 September. During most of the campaign, the model attributes most of the coarse mode concentrations to marine aerosols. This is consistent with the analysis of \citet{kojoj_arctic_2024}, who showed that coarse mode aerosols measured during MOCCHA were usually well-correlated with local wind speed, indicating a potential local source from open water near the ship.
        During major observed spikes (e.g., around August 21, September 13), WRF-Chem captures some, but not all, of the signal. Since the coarse mode measurements do not exclude local pollution from the ship, it is possible that the highest peaks correspond to local pollution not included in our simulations, especially during the ice-breaking periods before August 15 and September 8. Between August 28 and September 4, the model reproduces a strong transport event, reaching 10  \si{\ug\per\cubic\metre} in the observations and the model, and that the model attributes to mineral dust.

        Previous comparisons of model results at Arctic coastal measurement stations showed very good agreement for sea salt and total particulate matter. This means that the general underestimation is either due to an incorrect representation of local sources or to incorrect local removal or mixing processes during aerosol transport in the Arctic ocean. A version of the model including a specific treatment of emissions from sea ice leads exists and was recently developed by \citet{lapere_modeling_2024}, but integrating it with our own model developments was unfortunately not possible in time for this work. It is possible that the imprecise representation of local sea salt emissions explain the underestimation in our model. However, even if it is the case, our results indicate that organic and mineral dust aerosols, while potentially important for INPs, might only constitute a small fraction of the observed coarse aerosols, and are effectively not well constrained by the coarse aerosol observations. This means that dedicated measurement of dust and organic aerosols in the central Arctic would be necessary to better constrain INP parametrisations in this region.

        The differences are up to three orders of magnitude, with observed INPs active at \tempc{-10} averaging around \numrange{1e-4}{1e-3} during winter, compared to \numrange{1e-2}{1e-1} during August.
        The difference is weaker at lower temperatures.
        As for the modelled INPs, the difference with the previous period is more striking: the continuity of the series are broken by short peaks that reach the observations magnitude. Between those events of high concentrations, the modelled INPs happen to be much lower than what we observed during the MOCCHA period. It is not clear if  the measurement technique used during MOSAiC is able to reproduce such low concentrations or if they are instead treated as missing data, which could be the case seeing the gaps in the measurement series.
        This lowest background level is likely due to restrained emissions, from the extended winter sea ice and snow covers inhibiting MPOA and dust emissions respectively. It is also possible that INP removal is increased in winter due to efficient activation at ambient air temperatures. Quantifying the role of these different factors is made possible by our new approach, and could be investigated in future work.
        Peaks of concentrations that match the observations would instead be associated with propitious transport events.
        The best agreement for modelled concentrations happens at \tempc{-25}, where, despite a low background concentration, many peaks match the observations.
        This high variability could be linked to the differences in INP emissions relatively to temperature, or to events of in-cloud activation that happen because the air temperature decreases down to the activation threshold of the warmest INPs.

\subsection{The particle-to-INP approach}
\label{sec:particle_to_INP}
    The modelled dust particles turn out to be much lower than the observed coarse-mode concentrations (\cref{fig:coarse_concentrations} C), except in specific periods. But once converted into INPs by the ice nucleation schemes (\cref{fig:icenuc_params_moccha} A), the output concentrations catch the right order of magnitude.
    When the observed coarse-mode particles are used as input to the dust specific nucleation schemes, the INP concentrations are systematically overestimated.
    These results suggest that (1)~the coarse-mode is not dominated by dust, and (2)~the modelled dust order of magnitude could still be consistent with INP measurements. However, the spread among the dust parametrisations prevents to conclude on which perform better.
    On the other hand, the dust-specific D15 scheme gives very low concentrations when used with the modelled dust as input, but shows the best results when using the coarse-mode observations, suggesting, if this parametrisation is accurate, that the modelled dust could be underestimated and the coarse-mode essentially dust. But this is not consistant with the observations of \citet{kojoj_arctic_2024} who conclude on a coarse-mode likely dominated by particles from local sea spray. This indicates that D15 probably underestimates the INP activity of dust.
    The whole coarse-mode parametrisation D10, although it shows the lowest variability, gives the best order of magnitude, especially with the modelled particles. Fed with the observed coarse-mode particles, it overestimates by one order of magnitude the INP concentrations, but sticks to it at every temperatures, suggesting that non-species specific approaches remain better for first order estimations, although they are less physically based.

    Regarding the conversion of organic particles to INPs, the high concentrations of modelled PBAPs and MPOAs allow for the CNT PBAP and the W15 schemes to produce INP levels that almost reach the observations (\cref{fig:icenuc_params_moccha} A).
    Nevertheless, when using the observations of fPBAPs, the schemes are unanimously not efficient enough to produce INPs at the observed level.
    Figure \ref{fig:icenuc_params_organics} takes over panel B of \cref{fig:icenuc_params_moccha} for the organic specific schemes only. It also displays the fPBAP concentrations series used by the parametrisations. 
    It clearly shows that the observed INPs correlate with the fPBAPs, both in variability and values. This shared order of magnitude would imply a fPBAP activation fraction close to 100\% in order to explain the whole INP volume, but as shown on the figure, no nucleation scheme allows such high activation.
    In the active site density approach (used by H18 and M18), the size dependence is a parameter of an exponential which tends toward zero (c.f. \cref{eq:Ninp_inas}). %with Np the number of aerosols particles, Sp their surface area, and INAS the ice nucleating surface site density. 
    The term $1 - \text{exp}(-S_p\,n_s)$ is simply a factor to model the activation fraction. When $S_p$ becomes high enough, the exponential tends toward zero and the activation fraction reaches one.
    Because the observations on which the parametrisation is based show that \perc{100} activation does not happen \citep{hummel_simulating_2018}, the whole equation is capped by an extra factor that represent the maximum activation possible. In the case of H18B, for the \textit{P. Syringae} bacteria, this factor is set to $\gamma = 0.028$.
    Taking everything into account, when $S_p$ is high, the activation fraction tends toward $1\times0.028$ (i.e. \perc{2.8}). That explains why the parametrisation can not produce the same number of INP as the number of PBAPs.

    As stated by \citet{kojoj_arctic_2024}, there were a few or even no \textit{P. Syringae} in the measurements. Since \textit{P. Syringae} is considered as a very efficient biological particle for ice nucleation to our knowledge \citep{maki_ice_1974, joly_ice_2013, amato_survival_2015}, the results of H18B can be interpreted as a upper limit of the fPBAP conversion to INPs.
    Nonetheless, because other indicators state on the importance of PBAPs for INP production, it may mean that we are in the presence of particles far more efficient, which would motivate a new PBAP to INP parametrisation.

    \subsection{Emissions of INP tracers} 
    \label{sec:emissions_of_inp_tracers}
    % the absence of removal processes is a big lack, but it is corrected in the 2nd approach
    As mentioned before, an important drawback of the first approach is the absence of in-cloud removal of the particles considered as potential INPs; only dry deposition and washout were applied.
    The second approach addresses this problem by exposing the INP tracers to removal by in-cloud activation. The tracers are thus deposited when they meet the necessary temperature and cloudiness conditions for their activation as INP (c.f. \cref{sec:method_inp_emission}). 
    Once the tracers are interpolated, this has the effect of increasing the time variability of the concentration compared to the modelled INP series of the first approach, especially during the winter period of the MOSAiC campaign.
    This high variability has made the comparison with observations difficult, but it seems like this second approach agrees better with the observed level and variability of the observed INP time series.
    The deviations from observations are much lower than what the first approach has given, with values that stay within a single order of magnitude around the summer observations of MOCCHA. During winter, with the MOSAiC data, the underestimation of modelled INPs gets greater than two orders of magnitude almost exclusively when the observations lacks of data, and this lack of coverage might be due to INP being below the instrument's detection limit. In other words, the ebbs of the modelled series may correctly simulate low INP concentrations.   
    
    During summer 2018, the modelled INPs were too low at warm temperatures, and tended to be too high at colder temperatures. Since organic parametrisations are capped around \tempc{-15}, this would suggest issues from the dust scheme: it may be to efficient at cold temperatures, and not enough at warmer temperatures.
    This is particularly highlighted by the reproduction of the first INP peak (August 8\--15) which is reproduced only at \tempc{-25}.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion_chap4}
    % what was the link with the previous chapter
    In \cref{chap3} we have learned about the origins of the INPs that populate the Arctic atmosphere. Given the modelling focus of this thesis, the next step was to draw insights from these findings and undertake the modelling of the particles responsible for Arctic INPs.
    % what was at stake
    The INPs studied in \cref{sec:INP_sources} have been shown to originate from high latitude sources: erodible regions source of dust particles, open ocean areas which can emit marine primary organic aerosols from sea spray production, and possible contributions of remoter terrestrial areas origins of primary biological particles.

    % what we set up to answer the question
    In order to take into account these potential sources of INPs, as well as to continue the investigation of their validity, we implemented new high latitude emissions of these aerosol species.
    High latitude erodibility data allowed to extend the dust emission sources up to the shores of the Arctic ocean.
    Marine chlorophyll-a data was used to model emissions of MPOA from the ocean. 
    Finally, the large continental areas that circle the Arctic were parametrised as the emission origins of PBAPs (bacteria and fungal spores), known to be active INPs at high temperatures.
    In parallel, we used measurement data of particles and INPs, from central Arctic campaigns, in order to expose the model to observations.
    %%We tried to make the comparison as faithful as possible by 
    We adopt two different ways of modelling INP concentrations. The first used modelled and observed coarse-mode aerosol particles to feed immersion ice nucleation schemes. 
    Our second and novel approach went a step further by setting up emissions of INPs directly in the model. It used ice nucleation schemes to convert emitted particles into INPs, which were then transported by the model dynamics.

    % what was revealed
    In the comparison with observations from the MOCCHA 2018 campaign, the modelled dust particles were found to be in lower concentrations than the total observed coarse-mode. And in the first modelling approach, when the observed coarse-mode was used as input for dust particles parametrisations, the INP concentrations obtained were systematically higher than the observations, whereas modelled dust converted to INPs happened to produce plausible orders of magnitude.
    These observations tend to demonstrate that the MOCCHA coarse-mode was essentially not composed of dust, and that modelled dust might be correctly estimated.
    The all coarse-mode parametrisation of \citet{demott_predicting_2010} showed its efficiency in predicting INP concentrations levels. It did not reproduce the variability of the observations, but gave the best magnitude estimations across all activation temperatures.

    The modelled organic particles used with CNT and the \citet{wilson_marine_2015} parametrisation gave INP concentrations compatible with what has been observed.
    But the ice nucleation parametrisations used with the observation of fPBAPs systematically produced much too low INP levels, ruling out the possibility that fPBAP are driving INP concentrations. This result stands, unless the so-called fPBAP are potential INPs much more efficient than what is known yet. In this case, deriving a new parametrisation from these data would be much encouraged.
    The SSA to INP \citet{mccluskey_marine_2018} parametrisation, when used with the whole observed coarse-mode, gave good results at \tempc{-20} and \tempc{-25}, supporting the hypothesis of a coarse-mode of marine origin. The lack of good representation of warm INPs (active at \tempc{-15}), reinforces the motivation for a new PBAP parametrisation.

    The second modelling approach considered the in-cloud activation of INPs during their transportation. This allowed to better represent the variability of the observations, as well as their concentration drops.
    The general performances of this method are better than those of the first approach, which provides confidence in the maps of INP spatial distributions. The four-dimensional fields of INP concentrations for different activation temperatures given by this approach appear to make it a powerful tool in preparation for a coupling with the model microphysics.
    \vspace{\baselineskip}

    % bridge to next chapter
    In the next chapter, we will surf the wave of this modelling set-up, pushing toward a coupling of modelled INPs and cloud microphysics. We will set up a comparison experiment for the assessment of the impacts of INPs on Arctic cloud properties and their radiative effect. 


    \vspace{\baselineskip}
    \boxKR{Modelling of Arctic INPs}{
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Modelled organic particles used with adequate ice nucleation parametrisations give plausible INP concentrations.
            \item Measured concentrations of fPBAP converted into INPs with ice nucleation parametrisations give too low INP levels in order to support the hypothesis of PBAP-driven INPs.
            \item Emission of INP tracers is an efficient approach to reproduce consistent levels of INP concentrations, and enables visualisation of their spatial repartition.
            \item Concentrations of $10^{-1} \si{\per\liter}$ are rare in central Arctic for INP active at temperatures warmer than \tempc{-20}.
        \end{itemize}
