\section{Introduction and state of the science}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:intro_chap4}

\todo{Short intro sentence to link with the previous chapter}
 
% ice cloud in polar regions
The climate of polar regions, where ice-containing clouds are predominant \citep{matus_role_2017}, is highly subject to atmospheric ice particles.
Indeed, the latter determine the physical properties of clouds by exerting a strong influence on physical cloud properties such as lifetime and phase partitioning, which will therefore influence precipitations and radiative transfer \citep{cantrell_production_2005}.
Cloud ice systematically originates from a nucleation event, which can itself be of different nature. In homogeneous nucleation, the freezing rate depends on temperature and water vapour super-saturation, and happens without the assistance of any foreign substance \citep{vali_technical_2015}.
The initial nucleation event can be followed by secondary processes that magnify the ice production \citep{ickes_classical_2015}. 
In mixed-phase clouds (MPCs), the supercooled liquid water can thus turned into ice through the Wegener-Bergeron-Findeisen (WBF) effect \citep{korolev_limitations_2007}.

% introduction to hetero nucleation and INPs
In the Arctic, where the temperature at cloud height do not systematically reach the homogeneous ice nucleation threshold of $-38$ \unit{\degree C}, the particles responsible for the heterogeneous ice formation are critical. These ice nucleation particles (INPs), can be of various natures. 
In the remote region of high Arctic, the natural sources predominate over the anthropogenic emissions \citep{hartmann_variation_2019}. 
The main types of particles are mineral dust from desert, river bed, shores and glacial valley, and biological matter from vegetation debris and oceanic sea sprays. REFERENCE

% what are the important particles for INPs
The aerosol particles' ability to nucleate ice highly depends on their nature, which will affect the nucleation temperature and rate \citep{murray_ice_2012, hoose_heterogeneous_2012, kanji_overview_2017}.
Because of their nucleation ability due to their large sizes, and because of their high emission rates, coarse-mode mineral dust particles are often recognised as the most important type of INP globally \citep[][]{kok_mineral_2023}. 
But in remote marine environments such as central Arctic, this predominance is questioned, and marine biogenic particles could get the upper hand over the dust \citep{burrows_ice_2013}, although dust remain a strong contributor over marine sources \citep[][]{si_concentrations_2019}.

Additionally, biogenic particles make INPs active at warmer temperatures than dust.
They can be of various types: fungal spores, virus, bacteria, vegetation debris, and can originate from a broad range of environments \citep{despres_primary_2012}: marine sources \citep[][]{demott_sea_2016, creamean_ice_2019, irish_ice_2019, ickes_ice-nucleating_2020, kojoj_arctic_2024}, terrestrial vegetation \citep[][]{pereira_freitas_regionally_2023} and soil \citep[][]{creamean_thawing_2020, jensen_linking_2025}. 
Biological species that are particularly effective in ice nucleation, such as the \textit{Pseudomonas Syringae} bacteria that is among the most common species found in clouds \citep{vaitilingom_long-term_2012}, can trigger ice formation from $-3$ \unit{\degree C} \citep{maki_ice_1974, amato_survival_2015} and below $-24$ \unit{\degree C} \citep{vogel_ice-nucleating_2024}. In comparison, the K-Feldspar, most studied mineral for ice nucleation, does not efficiently nucleate at temperatures above $-12$ \unit{\degree C} \citep{connolly_studies_2009, ansmann_influence_2008}.
% the sources of INPs remain blurry
The contributions of the different types of aerosols to INPs remain uncertain. Many studies have tried to identify main sources of INP, but converging results are rare.
Furthermore, the tracing of INP sources as performed in the literature is not always reliable and rarely homogeneous \citep[][]{da_silva_how_2025}.
    
% what is the problematic?
    % knowledge on INPs
    In the frame of an accelerating global warming, and especially with the Arctic suffering polar amplification \citep{serreze_processes_2011, rantanen_arctic_2022, taylor_process_2022}, the efforts to better model the climate get intensified.
    Considering the importance of ice clouds in this context, investigating the role of INPs is crucial \citep{murray_opinion_2021}.
    The representation of INPs in models has even be suggested to be a key variable in the modelling of Arctic amplification \citep[][]{tan_potential_2022}.
    
    % modelling INPs
    Work on ice nucleation modelling goes back to the mid 20th century with the immersion freezing parametrisation of \citet{bigg_formation_1953}, the investigation on contact freezing by \citet{young_role_1974} and on active site density by \citet{fletcher_active_1969}, and more recently with the implementation of condensation and deposition freezing in a cloud model by \citet{meyers_new_1992}.
    In the last decades, the legacy of these studies has been accompanied by an increasing number of measurement campaigns. It brought new knowledge for species and region specific parametrisations \citep[e.g.][]{demott_predicting_2010, hoose_classical-theory-based_2010, niemand_particle-surface-area-based_2012, burrows_ice_2013, demott_integrating_2015, wilson_marine_2015, li_predicting_2022, gjelsvik_using_2025}.
    % efforts of implementation in models
    In parallel, efforts are made to implement this knowledge on ice nucleation modelling within aerosol-aware atmospheric models \citep[][]{gettelman_global_2010, hummel_simulating_2018, li_predicting_2022, kawai_dominant_2023, gjelsvik_using_2025}.
    The conclusions of these experiments highly vary from a study to another.
    With a dust focus, \citet{kawai_dominant_2023} emphasise the dominant role of local dust particles for Arctic INPs; results corroborated by the PBAP focused study by \citet{hummel_simulating_2018}. \citet{mccluskey_numerical_2019} have shown that modelled INPs from dust and sea sprays can efficiently reproduce observation in the North eastern Atlantic.
    In parallel, \citet{chatziparaschos_assessing_2025} highlight the seasonal and regional variability of the dominant contribution to INPs between dust and PBAPs from marine and terrestrial sources.
    
    % observation perspective
    From the observation perspective, both marine and terrestrial PBAPs appear to be of greater importance for Arctic INPs. 
    \citet{kojoj_arctic_2024} demonstrate the importance of fluorescent PBAPs (fPBAPs) during peaks of INP concentrations in the central Arctic, while \citet{barry_bioaerosols_2025} show the important contribution of bioaerosols for INPs in the same region.
    This discrepancy between the modelling and observational point of views fuels the questioning about what type of particles drive the INP populations in the Arctic. 
    
    
% declaration about what we want to achieve here
In this study, we use modelling tools to investigate the role of high latitude primary aerosols particles for the INP Arctic populations, with a focus on the respective contributions of PBAPs and mineral dust. Relying on both observations and modelling, we try to reconcile the both perspectives.
    
%    Demott 2010 says observations can not be extrapolated to some other places.
%  [terminology by vali 2015]

\vspace{\baselineskip}
% what we do in this study
The Weather Research and Forecasting model including chemistry (WRF-Chem) is used, along with a set of ice nucleation parametrisations, in order to 1)~reproduce in situ INP observations, 2)~investigate the effect of in-cloud activation on INP concentrations, 3)~get insights on the INP spatial distribution.
    % what is set up
    In a first off-line approach, different ice nucleation parametrisations are applied to both modelled and observed coarse mode aerosols particles. The modelled aerosols are composed of mineral dust particles emitted in mid and high latitude sources, including Iceland, Northern Canada, and Northern Russia. Marine primary organic aerosols (MPOA) also contribute to the coarse mode modelled particles. Those are emitted along with sea sprays in open ocean regions, and have a dependence with the marine biological activity.  
    The observed particles are from the Microbiology-Ocean-Cloud-Coupling in the High Arctic (MOCCHA) 2018, and the Multidisciplinary Drifting Observatory for the Study of Arctic Climate (MOSAiC) 2020 \citep{frickenhaus_data_2022} Arctic campaigns. Among the coarse particles, are distinguished the primary biological aerosol particles (PBAP) from the total.
    The INP observations used to compare the modelling results are also part of the campaigns datasets.
    
    In a second online\---but not cloud coupled\---approach, INP tracers are directly emitted from the aerosol sources. The activation temperatures are binned with a 5 degrees interval and range from $-5$ \unit{\degree C} to $-25$ \unit{\degree C}.
    The INP tracers are then dynamically transported during the simulation, and are subject to dry and wet deposition, as well as removal by in-cloud activation.
    This second setup allows to reproduce time series of INP concentrations that are comparable to the first approach's and to the observations'. But it also enables the quantification of in-cloud removal processes, as well as the investigation of the spatial distribution of INPs and of the emission sources contributions. 
    Finally, it opens the door to the implementation of online aerosol-cloud interactions which would benefit of the same investigation flexibility that this modelling setup offers.

% the plan
In a first section, the modelling tools are presented with detailed description of coarse-mode aerosols modelled emissions.
Then, the observational data from the two campaigns are described.
To end with the methods, the ice nucleation parametrisations used in this study is described at the light of the reason they have been chosen for our experiments.
The results section splits in two subsections: first the off-line modelling approach with modelled and observed aerosols converted to INPs, and secondly, the online approach with INP tracers emissions.
Finally, we discuss the results of the two approaches and the conclusions they tend to draw.

    IO2023 includes a dependency on SST but is based on a whitecap fraction approach. % explain what it is

    \subsection{Continental primary biological aerosols particles}
    \label{sec:pbap_emiss}
    Primary biological aerosols particles (PBAPs) can be of various different species. In this study, we model PBAPs from ocean regions as MPOA (c.f. Sect. \ref{sec:seaspray_emiss}), and from the continent as bacteria and fungal spores.
    The fungal spores emissions follow \citet{hummel_regional-scale_2015} parametrisation, which relies on temperature and specific humidity conditions, together with the leaf area index (LAI). The LAI is constructed from the Global Landcover 2000 Database (GLC2000).
    The emission flux density of fungal spores $F_{\text{FS}}$, expressed in \si{\per\square\metre\per\second}, is given by the following equation:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:fungal_spores}
        F_{\text{FS}} = b_1\, T\text{°C}+b_2\, q_v\, \text{LAI}
    \end{equation}
    where $T$ is the surface temperature in degrees Celsius, $qv$ the specific humidity in \si{kg\per kg}, and LAI the leaf area index in \si{\square\metre\per\square\metre}.
    
    The bacteria emissions is based on the parametrisation from \citet{burrows_bacteria_2009}.
    It relies on bacteria emission rates dependent of the surface types. The latter are derived from the MODIS surface categories. The details of the lumped ecosystems classes and the associated emission rates are given in the supplementary materials of the original paper \citep[][]{burrows_bacteria_2009}.
    
    The emission fluxes of the dust particles, the MPOA from sea sprays, and the continental PBAPs (fungal spores and bacteria), are illustrated on \cref{fig:emission_maps}.

    \subsection{Ice nucleation modelling } 
    \label{sec:inp_param}
    Ice nucleation parametrisations convert aerosol particles concentrations to number concentrations of INP on a scale of activation temperature.
    In this study, we stick to the representation of the immersion mode of nucleation.
    % Types of nucleation schemes
    Nucleation schemes can rely on various different approaches. Some of them are fitted temperature dependent functions of INP observations that directly give a number concentration of INP. They are therefore valid for a specific temperature range and particle type that correspond to the initial observations, and have strong dependence on the particle size distribution \citep[e.g.][]{demott_predicting_2010, demott_integrating_2015, wilson_marine_2015, li_predicting_2022}.
    Others use a slightly more complex approach, based on ice nucleation active surface site (INAS) density, also derived from observations. This approach assume that freezing happens at specific sites on the particle \citep{fletcher_active_1969, vali_quantitative_1971}. The nucleation depends on the aerosol particle surface area, rather than just on its number concentration. The INAS densities are determined with fitted experiments or measurements, and are consequently dependent on the particles properties \citep[e.g.][]{connolly_studies_2009, murray_ice_2012, niemand_particle-surface-area-based_2012, ullrich_new_2017, hummel_simulating_2018, mccluskey_marine_2018}.
    The standard equation of this approach has the following forms:
    \begin{equation}
        N_{\text{INP}} = N_{p} \left( 1 - \text{exp}(-S_{p}\, n_s)\right)
        \label{eq:Ninp_inas}
    \end{equation}
    with $N_p$ the number of aerosols particles, $S_p$ their surface area (\si{\meter\square}), and $n_s$ is the ice nucleating surface site density (\si{\per\meter\square}). The latter is where the different parametrisations apply.
    These parametrisations make the assumption that nucleation is time independent. 
    
    A contrario, the classical nucleation theory (CNT), takes a deeper step into the physics of ice crystal formation, considering ice nucleation as a stochastic process \citep{pruppacher_microphysics_1998, ickes_classical_2015}.
    More complex than the previous approaches, the CNT considers many factors, including thermodynamic parameters and particles properties. Those parameters can be difficult to determine \citep[e.g.][]{chen_parameterizing_2008, barahona_ice_2012, hoose_heterogeneous_2012, savre_theory-based_2015}, and the implementation of such an approach is heavier than the previous methods.
    
    % parameters considered for the different species
    Ice nucleation from PBAPs is considered to be driven by \textit{Pseudomonas syringae} from the modelled bacteria population, and by \textit{Mortierella alpina} for the modelled fungal spores (c.f. Sect. \ref{sec:pbap_emiss}), because these two species are particularly efficient at warm temperatures.
    The ice nucleation parametrisations used for these species are from \citet{hummel_simulating_2018}, with an INAS density approach.

    \subsubsection{Parameters of the INAS parametrisations}
    In the following sections, the INAS based parametrisations will be used for the production of INP tracers. Here, are described in details how they are set up.

    The number of INPs is given by Eq.~\ref{eq:Ninp_inas}. The INAS term is specific to every parametrisation, but each follows an equation of the form:
    \begin{equation}
        n_s(T) = \text{exp}(\alpha\,T + \beta) 
        \label{eq:inas}
    \end{equation}
    The number of particle $N_p$ of \cref{eq:Ninp_inas} can also be modulated by a factor $\epsilon$ that represents the abondance of potential INPs among the considered particles, and by $\gamma$, the fraction of maximum activation at low temperature for the considered species.
    These parameters are all derived from observations, and listed in \cref{tab:inas_param}.

            H18FS & \num{0.37573} & \num{4.23229} & \num{0.00851} & \num{0.08} \\

The exponential form of the INAS parametrisation makes it tends toward a plateau, of which the magnitude is driven by the $gamma$ parameter. 
The low $\gamma$ values of H18 parametrisations have the effect of capping the maximum activation fraction they can reach. 
Even if the activation fraction depends on both temperature and particle surface area, they cease to be effective once the plateau is reached.

Figure \ref{fig:activation_fraction} shows the evolution of the activation fraction for H18B, M18 and N12 against temperature and particle diameter.
All reach \perc{100} activation because no $\gamma$ factor is applied. The saturated zone on the left of the diagrams shows the parameter space (temperature and particle diameter) for which the parametrisations predict activation comprised within \perc{10} and \perc{100}. It happens more or less early, depending on the nature of the parametrisation. 
Once this threshold reached, either temperature nor particle diameter affect the activation efficiency.
For H18B (panel A), the considered particles which are bacteria, are known to be active at warm temperatures. Consequently, the parametrisation predicts high potential activation (later modulated by the $\gamma$ factor) relatively early, around \tempc{-10} for particles of \SI{1}{\micrometre}. 
Comparatively, it happens from \tempc{-27} for the dust particles in N12, considering the same particle size.

The inclinaison of the activation fraction step lines on the diagrams account for the weight of the two parameters (temperature and particle diameter). Vertical lines would indicate no effect of the size, while horizontal lines would mean no temperature dependence.
H18B is more sensitive to temperature than M18, and N12 shows the strongest dependence in particle diameter.
In term of modelling, the latter means that parametrisations sensitive to particle size will suffer more of imprecisions on the modelled particle size distributions.
For that matter, H18B is the less subject to particle size, and consequently, the most reliable INAS parametrisation.

    \subsection{INP emissions}
    \label{sec:method_inp_emission}
    This second approach uses the best performing ice nucleation parametrisations\----identified with the first approach\----to convert modelled dust, PBAPs (bacteria and fungal spores) and sea sprays into INPs, directly at emission.
    INP tracers are emitted from this conversion into five classes, differentiated by their activation temperature and ranged from \tempc{-5} to \tempc{-25}, with a five degrees Celsius increment.
    They are then transported as air tracers on which are applied rain washout, dry deposition, and they are subject to removal by in-cloud activation (see Sect. \ref{sec:removal}). % to be changed
    
    In order to convert aerosol emissions into INP tracers active at the temperature $T$, we use INAS based and species specific parametrisations set up at $T$.
    The INPs from dust particles are produced with the parametrisation by \citet{niemand_particle-surface-area-based_2012}.
    INPs from sea sprays can be produced with two different approaches. First, the organic mass fraction of sea sprays can be considered as active bacteria and converted into INPs with the \citet{hummel_simulating_2018} parametrisation. But the species analysis work concomitant to the study by \citet{kojoj_arctic_2024}, showed that this assumption is unlikely since the marine organic aerosols observed during MOCCHA were devoid of \textit{Pseudomonas syringae} bacteria.
    Alternatively, the total sea sprays can be used to produce INPs with the parametrisation from \citet{mccluskey_marine_2018}, which has shown good performances in reproducing INP concentrations when combined with dust parametrisations \citep[][]{mccluskey_numerical_2019}.
    We excluded the parametrisation from \citet{wilson_marine_2015} because of its poor performances demonstrated in \citet{mccluskey_numerical_2019}. %and verified with our setup.
    Table \ref{tab:inp_emiss} indexes the parametrisations used for the emissions of INPs by the different aerosol species.

    \subsection{INP removal and activation}
    \label{sec:removal}
    The main advantage of the INP tracers approach is the ability to monitor the activation of the INPs.
    % INP removal
    The activation mechanism is modelled as a removal of the INP tracers. In this study, we do not consider any post-activation recycling of INPs. Therefore, in the following, the terms "activation" and "removal" will be synonyms. 
    Nevertheless, this setup is suitable for testing INP recycling parametrisations.
    
    The temperature threshold is the main driver of the activation process. A temperature condition is thus used to remove the INPs of which the activation temperature meets the condition.
    The activation of $N_a$ INPs in the temperature class $T$ (i.e. INPs configured as active at the temperature $T$), will lead to the activation of the INPs classes that correspond to temperatures higher than $T$.
    The amount $N_a$ of INP removed is the same in all the temperature classes that meet the temperature criterion.
    $N_a$ can be modulated by different meteorological conditions, such as relative humidity, liquid water content or cloud fraction. % what do we use ? 
    In the setup presented in this study, the removal of INPs rely on the model cloud fraction:
    \begin{equation}
        N_a = N_{INP} * (1-f_{cloud})
    \end{equation}
    with $N_{INP}$ the number of INPs active at the temperature $T$ and above, and $f_{cloud}$ the model cloud fraction.
    
    % activation in clouds
    If one wants to use this approach for aerosol-cloud interactions modelling, the activation parametrisation needs more attention, since it will be linked to the cloud microphysics model schemes.
    This is not in the scope of the present study.

\subsection{Learnings from the particles to INPs approach}
    The modelled dust particles turn out to be much lower than the observed coarse-mode concentrations (\cref{fig:coarse_concentrations} C). But once converted into INPs by the ice nucleation schemes (\cref{fig:icenuc_params_moccha} A), the output concentrations happened to catch the right order of magnitude.
    When the observed coarse-mode particles are used as input of the dust specific nucleation schemes, the INP concentrations are systematically overestimated.
    These results suggest that (1) the coarse-mode is not dominated by dust, (2) the modelled dust might present a coherent order of magnitude. However, the spreading among the dust parametrisations prevents to conclude on which perform better.
    On the other hand, the dust specific D15 scheme gives very low concentrations with the modelled dust, but shows the best results when using the coarse-mode observations, suggesting the modelled dust would be underestimated and the coarse-mode essentially dust. But this is not consistant with the observations of \citet{kojoj_arctic_2024} who conclude on a coarse-mode dominated by organic particles.
    The whole coarse-mode parametrisation D10, although it shows the lowest variability, gives the best order of magnitude, especially with the modelled particles. Fed with the observed coarse-mode particles, it overestimates by one order of magnitude the INP concentrations, but sticks to it at every temperatures, suggesting that non-species specific approaches remain better for first order estimations, although they are less physical.

    Concerning the conversion of organic particles to INPs, the high concentrations of modelled PBAPs and MPOAs allow for the CNT PBAP and the W15 schemes to produce INP levels that almost reach the observations (\cref{fig:icenuc_params_moccha} A).
    Nevertheless, when using the observations of fPBAPs, the schemes are unanimously not efficient enough to produce INPs at the observed level.
    Figure \ref{fig:icenuc_params_organics} takes over panel B of \cref{fig:icenuc_params_moccha} for the organic specific schemes only. It also displays the fPBAP concentrations series used by the parametrisations. 
    It is clearly shown that the observed INPs correlate with the fPBAPs, both in variability and values. This shared order of magnitude would imply a fPBAP activation fraction close to one in order to explain the whole INP volume, but as shown on the figure, no scheme allows it.
    In the active site density approach (used by H18 and M18), the size dependence is a parameter of an exponential which tends toward zero (c.f. \cref{eq:Ninp_inas}). %with Np the number of aerosols particles, Sp their surface area, and INAS the ice nucleating surface site density. 
    The term $1 - \text{exp}(-S_p\,n_s)$ is simply a factor for modelling the activation fraction. When $S_p$ becomes high enough, the exponential tends toward zero and the activation fraction reach one.
    Because the observations on which the parametrisation is based show that \perc{100} activation do not happen \citep{hummel_simulating_2018}, the whole equation is capped by an extra factor that represent the maximum activation possible. In the case of H18B, for the \textit{P. Syringae} bacteria, this factor is set to $\gamma = 0.028$.
    Taking everything into account, when $S_p$ is high, the activation fraction tends toward $1\times0.028$ (i.e. \perc{2.8}). That explains why the parametrisation can not produce the same number of INP as the number of PBAPs. 
    Is this physical? Parametrisations are intended to describe observations as faithfully as possible. In the original paper, it does. Here, their results have been applied to a different case, and it may does not suit the situation.
    As stated by \citet{kojoj_arctic_2024}, there was a few or even no \textit{P. Syringae} in the measurements. Since \textit{P. Syringae} is considered as a very efficient biological particle for ice nucleation to our knowledge \citep{maki_ice_1974, joly_ice_2013, amato_survival_2015}, the results of H18B can be interpreted as a upper limit of the fPBAP conversion to INPs.
    Nonetheless, because other indicators state on the importance of PBAPs for INP production, it may mean that we are in the presence of particles far more efficient, which would motivate a new PBAP to INP parametrisation.

    Although, the M18 parametrisation applied to fPBAPs only gives very little INPs (which is expected since it is intended to be used with SSA), when applied to all coarse-mode, it produces levels that are consistent with the INP observations at \tempc{-20} and \tempc{-25}.

    These observations motivate the development of a new parametrisation specific to this particular case, for which no existing ice nucleation parametrisation satisfyingly reproduce the observed INPs.

    \subsection{What the emission of INP tracers has revealed} 
    % the absence of removal processes is a big lack, but it is corrected in the 2nd approach
    As mentioned before, an important drawback of the first approach is the absence of in-cloud removal of the particles considered as potential INPs; only dry deposition and washout were applied.
    The second approach addresses this problem by exposing the INP tracers to removal by in-cloud activation. The tracers are thus artificially deposited when they meet the necessary temperature and cloudiness conditions for their activation as INP (c.f. \cref{sec:method_inp_emission}). 
    Once the tracers are interpolated, this has the effect of increasing the time variability of the concentration compared to the modelled INP series of the first approach, especially during the winter period of the MOSAIC campaign.
    This high variability has made difficult the comparison with observations, but many matchs stood out.
    The deviations from observations are much lower than what the first approach has given, with values that stay within a single order of magnitude around the summer observations of MOCCHA. During winter, with the MOSAIC data, the underestimation of modelled INPs gets greater than two orders of magnitude almost exclusively when the observations lacks of data. In other words, the ebbs of the modelled series may correctly simulate low INP concentrations.   
    
    During summer 2018, the modelled INPs were too low at warm temperatures, and tended to be too high at colder temperatures. Since organic parametrisations are capped around \tempc{-15}, this would suggest a cause from the dust scheme: it may be to efficient at cold temperatures, and not enough at warmer temperatures.
    This is particularly highlighted by the reproduction of the first INP peak (August 8\--15) which is reproduced only at \tempc{-25}.
