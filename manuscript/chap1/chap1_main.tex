%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This is the early morning of month September 1572. A slight breeze blows through the birches foliages of Herrevad Abbey, yet untouched by the sea sprays of the Öresund strait, just fifty kilometres westward.
Merging with the whisper of aspen leaves, the friction sound made by the moving discs of Sophia Brahe's astrolabe alternates with the rustle of her observation notebook pages. The latter, full of azimuth and declination coordinates immersed within orbit sketches, was a pearl among the Renaissance astronomers. 
Her elder brother had left a few hours ago aiming for his bed, but as for her, she could not leave the perfect conditions that this night offered. Her care and rigour, that she developed in astronomical observation and kept all her life, is what led the Brahe sister and brother to the discovery, a few month later, of the \textit{Tycho's supernovae}. Their observation of this "new star" hase been the cornerstone of the Aristotle's immutability of supralunar world dogma collapsing. 
But that night, Sophia's mind was wondering far from antiquity's theories about the Universe. She was trying to make the most of this new moon and no cloud night of late summer, with in mind the completion of two years of Mars' apparent retrograde motion tracking.
But when the sunlight started reaching the edge of horizon, Sophia got bothered in her last series of measurements. Even though she followed all night long the rise and sky cruising of the red planet, she just lose its track as it started its setting. 
First, she was tempted to blame her sleepy eyes, but she knew that the fresh morning breeze of Scania\----present-day southern Sweden\----always successfully keeps her well awake.
No, something more unusual was happening, and it had nothing to do with the young astronomer's eyes.
As she focused her gaze on the area where Mars should have been, she got intrigued by what she saw. No planet nor star was visible there, and it could have seem normal since the Sun was rising but, Venus and the Cancer constellation were still dominating the sky, although they were lower on the horizon. The image was very faint, but she could not be mistaken: the dark blue cloth of starry sky was masked by the perfectly round disk, yet very pale, of the full Moon.
How could it have appeared, or started to appear, while the whole night was clear of any moon? Sophia checked her notebook, looking for the lunar calendar she was keeping. No mistaking, the Moon had just finished her twenty height days cycle. It had to stay invisible for a whole day before growing again as a crescent, while the Sun-Moon-Earth triptych gets into favorable position. 
But there was no sign of any slight slice of young Moon. Instead, the round and well known face of the Earth's satellite was unveiling itself, as nocturnal prey birds casually cast their shadows after having caught their reckless meal within the forest humus.
Without understanding it fully, Sophia was looking at her own reflection. 
\vspace{\baselineskip}

It is hard to know if Sophia Brahe could actually be surprised by the appearance of a full Moon disk on a new Moon day. Despite the fact she is recognized as the great assistant of her famous brother\footnote{Tycho Brahe was a 16\up{th} century Danish astronomer and alchemist. He is known for the observation of the supernova SN1572, and for his extremely precise sky observations from his observatory of Uraniborg, although their were performed with the bare eye.}, the knowledge about her appreciation of physical causes stay barely not documented. However, her young age at the time\----her birth being known to take place between 1556 and 1559\----could allow the hypothesis of a teenager observing a new Moon for the first time, especially since this phenomenon is not as trivial as one could think.
Getting back to the basics: the Moon appears bright to us because its surface reflects the sunlight toward the Earth. The apparent shape of this reflection evolves as the Moon rolls around the Earth, affecting the overall configuration of this three bodies system.
When the Sun, the Earth and the Moon are aligned, with the planet standing in between, the lighted side of the Moon faces the earthlings who see it as a circular disk. Half a lunar cycle away to that full Moon configuration, stands the new Moon's. In fourteen days, the satellite has made its way to the other side of the Earth, which makes it the middle piece of the trio. The Sun now lights the back of the Moon, and no sunlight is reflected toward the Earth. 
Consequently, the new Moon nights are reputed to be perfect for astronomical observations, since only the observed objects are sources of light. The invisible Moon bothers no astronomer, unless it does...
Here lies the enigma that caused Sophia's astonishment when she saw the complete Moon's visible face during that no Moon night. She could not find Mars any more because it got behind the invisible Moon (which is itself, a pretty rare event). But when the Sun starts rising and therefore, lighting the Earth surface, the latter reflects partially the solar rays toward space. Some of this reflected sunlight eventually reach the Moon, making it appearing to any attentive observer, like was Sophia.
This ability of reflecting lights is called albedo. Measured as the fraction of reflected over incident short wave radiation (most known as visible light), the Earth albedo reaches the temporally and spatially averaged value of \num{0.3}. In other words, \SI{30}{\%} of the energy coming from the Sun and reaching the Earth is reflected by the latter's atmosphere and ground. Which lets \SI{70}{\%} of it be absorbed by the planet. 

This Sun's energy is how the Earth gets its power. It is the energy that fuels the great majority of the processes that happen on Earth. From the plants in their photosynthesis efforts, to the solar panels that mimics the vegetal genius, including the uplifting of air parcels that makes the wind blow, which, when combined with the evaporation of sea waters, makes thunder storms and hurricanes. 
This solar energy is not only the source of so-called renewable energies, but is at the root of the fossil fuels that have made the modern civilisation. 
\vspace{\baselineskip}

The first law of thermodynamics states that, in a closed system, the energy is conserved. Hopefully, the Earth is not a closed system, if only because of the energy it gets from the Sun. But in physics, an open system that gets energy without emitting any is closer to a boiler than to a vivarium. Therefore, since it is pretty uncommon to observe spontaneous bubbles in the Earth's oceans, one can assume that the planet is somehow getting rid of its energy.
When a third of the incoming energy is reflected thanks to the planetary albedo, the rest is re-emitted as radiative emissions. Following Planck's law of thermal emission, any body of non zero temperature emits electromagnetic radiations; the emission wavelength depending on the body's temperature.
The Earth's surface temperature under the reign of Humans averages around \SI{13.5}{\degreeCelsius}\footnote{The year 2024 was the first to reach \SI{1.5}{\degreeCelsius} above the \SI{13.7}{\degreeCelsius} of the 1850-1900 global average.}, which is in the middle of the infrared emission spectrum. Thus, the energy exits the system as infrared radiative emissions (IR).
Because both inward and outward energy fluxes happen respectively as short wave (SW) and long wave (LW) radiations, we often speak of the Earth's \textit{radiative budget}.
In order to stay at an overall constant temperature, the Earth's system has to emit \textit{exactly} the amount of energy received from the solar star. If the incoming energy amount (SW) is lower than the outgoing energy flux (LW), the planet gets cooler. Conversely, the slightest positive imbalance in the budget drives slowly but surely the system to warm (see \cref{sec:causes_of_GW} for a real conditions example), until the long wave radiations grow enough to match the incoming short wave radiations.
\vspace{\baselineskip}

From this perspective, an apparent paradox\----or rather a fundamental question\----arises. If the energy received from the Sun is entirely re-emitted into space, it is hard to consider energy as fuel\----since it is not consumed\----nor the Sun as a source. Consequently, the question is: what does the Earth actually get from its star?

We said herein above that the solar energy was the source of every movement, except geological, on Earth. But yet, the energy neither dissipates nor accumulates in the system: it stays constant by evacuating the exact amount simultaneously received. 
In order to lift the paradox, it is necessary to summon the second law of thermodynamics. First introduced by Sadi Carnot (1796-1832), and rectified by Rudolf Clausius (1822-1888), the Carnot's principle states that in any transformation, the energy implied changes its intrinsic qualities, losing some of its ability to transform. This lost, has been named \textit{entropy} by Clausius, and writes $S$. 
Later, Josiah Willard Gibbs (1839-1903) introduced the concept of free energy, or available energy. Noted $G$\----or $F$ when relating to the Hermann von Helmoltz (1821-1894) potential\----, the free energy points out the capability of physical transformation, and thus is related to low entropy states. Quantitatively, it is the maximum amount of work a thermodynamic system can produce. In the physics of phase transitions, $G$ is the key quantity.
In its modern form, the Carnot's principle, or second law of thermodynamics, states that, during any transformation within a closed system, the entropy can not decrease, and most likely increases. This implies the vanishing of free energy, replaced by bound energy.
While the first law teaches the conservation of energy, the second states its systematic \textit{degradation}. Unlike the Newtonian description of motion, the thermodynamics introduces an immutable irreversibility of physical transformations; it gives a one-way direction to Arthur Eddington's arrow of time.
Additionally, it echoes\----without contradicting it\----the Lavoisier's principle, first pronounced at the dawn of the first French republic. Its famous statement is resumed as follows: "Rien ne se perd, rien ne se crée, tout se transforme."\footnote{"Nothing is lost, nothing is created, everything is transformed."}. 
Even though it was deduced from chemical experiments, and despite the fact it remarkably suits the late 18\up{th} century French politics, in order to fully apply to energy it needs to be amended with the notion of degradation: "[...] se transforme et se dégrade.". One should hope that modern politics is not ruled by this updated version.

Thus, the Sun provides energy to the Earth of course, but the latter does not keep it. What the planet does actually get and consume is the low entropy provided by the star. Beyond being a formidable nuclear power plant, the Sun is our one and only source of free energy, helping the Earth keep its local entropy equilibrium. But it is only through life development that this free energy can turn into low entropy.

At his time, the physicist Erwin Schrödinger (1887-1961) was already throwing a critical eye on the way human societies were behaving toward Nature. Being recognized as a great physicist by his peers, he allowed himself to extend to biology his knowledge in physics. By looking at life on Earth, he understood that it was the embodiment of negative entropy, or \textit{negentropy}, in the Universe: life feeds on solar free energy to develop and grow in organisation (i.e. as \textit{organisms}), and doing so consume energy with local negative entropy balance.
\vspace{\baselineskip}

\section{The Anthropogenic disturbance}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:anthropo_disturbance}
\tquote{Human beings, who are almost unique in having the ability to learn from the experience of others, are also remarquable for their apparent disinclination to do so.}{Douglas Adams} 
% In the last second of the Day of the Universe (Carl Sagan), this is where a single species gets the power of astronomical or geological events.

In 1980, the astronomer and famous science populariser Carl Sagan, was introducing for the first time the Cosmic Calendar. The idea was to squeeze the entire history of the Universe\----from the Big Bang to nowadays\---- within one Earth year calendar. 
In this calendar, the Big Bang happens at the first second of January 1\up{st}, while my PhD viva voce takes place at exactly midnight on December 31\up{st}\footnote{That is not the actual date and time of the defence. Beware of mixing the Cosmic Calendar with the Gregorian one.}, one year later.
On that time scale, the Milky Way forms on May 6\up{th}, the Sun on August 31\up{st}. Fourteen days later the Earth forms, and only height days later the first life forms appear. But one has to wait until December 1\up{st} to see oxygen in the atmosphere. The reign on dinosaurs ends on the penultimate day of the year, and humans get differentiated from apes at 8 pm on the 31\up{st}. Homo Sapiens appears at 23:56, and finally Sophia Brahe was born at the beginning of the last second of 23:59.
The last four hundred years of human civilisation fits in the very last second of the Cosmic Calendar. The most astonishing scientific and technological progresses along with the deadliest wars happened during that last second. During half of that microscopic fraction of the Universe's history, the human population grew of one order of magnitude.
But most importantly, that last fraction of second sheltered an event that has never happened in the whole history of the Earth, or of any other planet as far as our knowledge goes. Over the last two hundred years, a single species reached the power, then overpassed it, of astronomical and geological forcers. The 21\up{st} century started with human societies capable of altering the climate and jeopardising greatly the life conditions on Earth.

In this section, we will go through the 2025 global warming state of play and the scientific evidences that support it. Then we will revue the main causes and mechanisms of the phenomenon and its expected evolution. Finally, we will discuss the implications of such an unprecedented experiment for our research practices and topics.

The climate is constituted of five components that interact continuously with each others: the atmosphere, or the layer of gas and in suspension particles that surrounds the planet; the hydrosphere, or the whole water that compose oceans, rivers, aquifers and clouds; the cryosphere, or everything that is frozen including sea ice, glaciers, snow cap and permafrost; the lithosphere, or the rigid envelop of the planet; and the biosphere or the whole living, from bacteria to humans, including all plants, animals and microorganisms.

This thesis will primarily focus on the atmosphere. Nevertheless, its interactions with the four others components of climate will be time to time at the heart of the subject.
The cryosphere will be the common thread, as the Arctic is the permanent landscape where the action takes place.
Since the tropospheric clouds are major actors in the following chapters, the hydrosphere will naturally accompany the general intention.
As for the lithosphere and biosphere, they will take action about aerosol particles compositions and sources, and will not exit the scene until the end.
In that way, this PhD work can be seen as a study of the interrelations of those five climatic actors, with a focus on the causal links they bear.

     --> schematics of the climate components 

% the radiative budget
Beyond the interactions within the climate system, the Earth interacts with its outer environnement, namely: outer space. As mentioned in the previous section, the Earth receives free energy from the Sun, and has to emit toward space the same amount of degraded (i.e. high entropy) energy.
This happens through what is called the radiative budget of the planet.
One third of the incoming solar radiations is reflected by the ground and the atmosphere (due to the albedo); the remaining is absorbed by the system. 
In response, the planet (through its five climate components) emits infrared radiations toward space. Except a small part\----that goes through what is called the atmospheric window\----, all this long wave radiation is absorbed by the atmosphere.
This absorption is due to greenhouse gas (GHG), of which, on Earth, the most important is water vapour. GHG are gas that have absorption rays in the telluric infrared spectrum, or in other words, they can absorb the long wave radiations emitted at Earth's temperature.
The atmosphere, warmed up by long wave absorption, then re-emits this energy in all directions, toward ground and outer space\footnote{It is interesting to notice that the actual power density of long wave emissions from the atmosphere toward ground is about \SI{324}{\watt\per\meter\square}, more or less the double of short wave incoming radiations at the surface.}.
However, an augmentation of GHG concentrations in the atmosphere makes it more opaque to long wave radiations, and creates an imbalance in the radiative budget: less energy can escape the system which leads to its warming.
This causes the system to drift toward a new energy equilibrium\----with a warmer average temperature\---- that would allows stronger long wave radiations capable of overcoming the additional infrared absorption by GHG.
At the first order, as long as the incoming solar radiations are stable (what they are), the GHG concentrations drives the average temperature on Earth.
Consequently, if the GHG concentrations keep on increasing, so would do the temperature of the new equilibrium.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), is a scientific body established to provide comprehensive assessments of the scientific, technical, and socio-economic information relevant to understanding human-induced climate change and its impacts. By critically evaluate the existing literature and synthesize findings to inform climate action by various stakeholders, including governments and international organizations, the IPCC serves as a critical platform for synthesizing climate science, fostering collaboration across disciplines, and providing evidence-based insights to guide global climate action. The IPCC is organized into three main working groups, each focusing on different aspects of climate change:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Working Group I (WG1): Concentrates on the physical science basis of climate change, assessing the scientific literature related to climate systems and their changes.

    \subsubsection{Drivers of climate variability}
    % intro about climate natural evolution
    The Earth's climate is by essence an evolutive system. It's complexity, as glimpsed in the previous sections, makes it a chaotic system that reaches equilibrium from time to time.
    Among the mere infinity of climate drivers, some of them deserve to be cited as the main causes of climate evolution. 
    First of all, the orbital parameters of the Earth have be theorised by Milutin Milanković, James Croll and Joseph-Alphonse Adhémar as the pacemaker of the climate \citep{hays_variations_1976}.
    % three parameters and their timescales
    The longest and most impactful orbital cycle find its source in the variation of the Earth's orbit eccentricity, with a main period of \SI{400000}{years}, and a secondary period of \SI{100000}{years}.
    The second is the obliquity (or inclinaison of the rotation axis with respect to the ecliptic plan\footnote{The plan of the Earth's (and most planets') orbit around the Sun.}) variation, which cycles every \SI{41000}{years} and acts on the seasons strength. A low obliquity causes a weak seasonality, while a strong one strengthen the seasonal gradient.
    Finally, the shortest is due to the equinox precession, with a period of \SI{26000}{years}, and can cause inequality in seasons magnitudes among the two hemispheres.
    The combinaison of these has caused the great climate variability over the last hundred of thousand years.
    These climate variations always have a main parameter, namely the surface temperature. Because all of the five climate components are sensitive to thermodynamics, the evolution of temperature causes a cascade of changes creating feedback loops. 
    Some of them can act as restoring force, preventing phenomena to grow strong, and are called \textit{negative} feedback loops.
    Others can fuel the phenomenon that initiated them, like vicious circle, or what is called a \textit{positive} feedback loops.
    
    % other major drivers
    Among other major drivers, tectonic activity and volcanism can induce non-cyclic climate forcing. Large eruptions inject significant amounts of ash and sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, leading to temporary cooling effects due to increased albedo and reduced solar radiation reaching the surface. Important injection of water vapour into the stratosphere can also happen during volcanic eruption. It is estimated that the 2022 eruption of Hunga Tunga underwater volcano blasted into the stratosphere 10\% of the total stratospheric water vapour, with complex effects on the climate \citep{chen_impact_2025}.
    Eruptions effects can last from a few days to a few months for the most common events, but very large eruptions have induced major changes to the past climate, with important consequences on living species. 
    
    % the Sun
    Eventually, the Sun forces periodic variations to the climate. Putting aside the short yearly and very short daily variations of solar radiation due to the orbital motion of the Earth, the Sun presents an activity cycle of eleven years in average. The associated variations in energy flux reaching the Earth's surface during a cycle is about \num{0.1} to \SI{0.2}{W m^{-2}}. Meanwhile, the radiative forcing induced by anthropogenic activities is estimated at \SI{2.7}{W m^{-2}} \citep{intergovernmental_panel_on_climate_change_climate_2023}.
    Longer solar cycles have been theorised and partially observed, and are candidates to explain past climate events. Nevertheless, the magnitude of such variations always stay around a tenth of a Watt per square meters, one order of magnitude lower than the current anthropogenic forcing.
    
    \subsubsection{The anthropogenic forcing}
    As mentioned in the preamble of this section, humanity has gotten, over the last hundred years, a power of action equivalent to the drivers presented herein above.
    Despite its extreme power of destruction on ecosystems (see \cref{sec:silent_collapse}), its main impact comes from its ability to change the atmospheric chemical composition. 
    Figure \ref{fig:keeling_curve} depicts the evolution, from the Gregorian calendar start up to nowadays, of the atmospheric concentration of carbon dioxide (\ce{CO2}). The curve shows how stable was this concentration over the past two thousand years. During the first three quarters of that period, civilisations have risen and collapsed, wars have roared, famine and plenty periods have succeeded one another. Notwithstanding, the curve remained still until the late 18\up{th}.
    What happened then is well known and understood: the first two industrial revolutions happened.

    The term industrial revolutions refers to the historical periods characterized by rapid industrial growth and profound socio-economic impacts. We call \textit{first} industrial revolution, the dramatic increase of England industrial production. Hold by the invention of the steam engine and the great English coal stocks, this period of great social changes brought England to the pole position of the country production ranking.
    After a few decades, the techniques got exported, and the revolution extended to Europe and later to various other nations, including the United States. This second industrial revolution marked the commencement of a substantial escalation in the utilization of fossil fuels.
    
    % words on the emissions of CO2 due to fossil fuels
    The combustion of hydrocarbures (which fossil fuels are), combines the dioxygen of air with the carbon of the long molecules of fuel. Among other chemical co-products of the combustion (including particulate matter), \ce{CO2} is emitted. 
    The absorption spectrum of \ce{CO2} contains rays in the infrared wavelengths, right into the emission spectrum of the telluric emissions of planet Earth. Therefore, the atmospheric \ce{CO2} tends to absorb and block the outgoing long wave radiations. 
    An increase of \ce{CO2} atmospheric concentration induces an imbalance in the radiative budget, causing a warming until a new equilibrium is reached. However, while the concentration is increasing, the radiative budget stays imbalanced and the warming goes on.
    This additional greenhouse effect is the root cause of the observed global warming.
    
    % fossil fuels and combustion
    Figure \ref{fig:energies} gives an overview of the annual \ce{CO2} emissions per region and activities (panel A), as well as the evolution of primary energy consumption by sources (panel B).
    Both graphs highlight the striking regime change after WWII. This acceleration was prepared by the slow but steady development of fossil fuels within the industry. Panel B shows that the increasing use of coal since the middle of the 19\up{th} century, has been joined by the rapid development of oil in the first half of the 20\up{th} century. Simultaneously, but slower, the natural gas\footnote{Not more natural than coal or oil, natural gas is a side product of the latter two, and therefore belong to fossil fuels as well.} rose in the global energy mix.
    
    It is commonly mistaken that primary energies replace each others, holding the idea of continuous and systematic technological improvement. But as illustrated by \cref{fig:energies} B, the energies place themselves on top of each others. \citet{fressoz_pour_2021} demonstrates that they not only pile up, but also stimulate the use of each others, in a symbiotic dynamic. 
    Coal development did not suppress wood consumption, but rather stabilised it because of the need of coal mines staying and railways for wood props. 
    Oil never replaced coal, but rather enhanced its production thanks to the development of terrestrial and marine transportation it allowed. Gas piled as an extra primary energy, but the inherent constraints of gas phase fluids technically prevents it to be largely used instead of oil or coal. Finally, the other energy vectors stay marginal, and have low to no contribution to the reduction efforts of global fossil fuel consumption. The new renewable energy vectors, namely solar and wind, even develop in a symbiotic relation with fossil fuels, as their production greatly relies on the oil, coal and gas industry.
    Nowadays, 80 \% of the energy used in the world come from fossil fuel combustion.

    \subsubsection{An unprecedented rate}
    % what is striking and unprecedented is the speed of the change (conclude on that)
    What makes the current global warming so special, is its rate. 
    In the Earth's history, climate warmings and cooling happened with magnitudes of one to ten degrees. Over the last \SI{800000}{years}, the Milanković cycles induced an alternance of long ice ages and short tempered periods like the Holocene we are in. At the last glacial maximum (LGM), \SI{21000}{years} ago, the average air surface temperature was of 9°C, or 4 to 5°C lower than the temperature of 1900. 
    The warming that brought the global temperature up to 13°C took \num{5000} to \SI{10000}{years} to happen.
    In comparison, the temperature rose of 0.75°C between 1900 and 2000; since year 2000 this warming almost doubled, flirting with 1.5°C. Those correspond to warming rates respectively seven and thirty times higher than the last deglaciation\footnote{Assuming a lower bound rate of 5°C over \SI{5000}{years}.}.

\subsection{Expected evolution}
\label{sec:expected_evolution}
% Short intro on the projection work of IPCC
Apart the work of understanding the past and current climate changes, the scientific community\----from which the IPCC pull their evidences\----also focus on climate forcasting. In other words, the IPCC reports have also the charge of depicting the face of the future climate from the last advancements in climate sciences.
In their last report, the AR6, the IPCC introduced a new scriptwriting tool called Shared Socio-economic Pathways (SSP). They are representative of the human societies responses to the global warming, and are complementary to the more classic Representative Concentration Pathways (RCP), which only consider the GHG emissions and the associated radiatif forcing. 
The SSP nomenclature works like the following: in SSn-X, X refers to the radiative forcing reached in 2100, expressed in Watts per square meters, similarly as the RCP convention.
The number n, account for the type of global collective response to the global warming, giving a narrative dimension to the scenario. Those are defined as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item{SSP1 – Sustainability:} This pathway envisions a world focused on sustainable development, characterized by inclusive growth, reduced inequalities, and a strong emphasis on environmental protection. It assumes significant progress in technology and education, leading to low greenhouse gas emissions and high resilience to climate impacts.
    \item{SSP2 – Middle of the Road:} This scenario represents a continuation of current trends, where socio-economic development follows a moderate trajectory. It combines elements of sustainability and fossil-fuel-based growth, resulting in moderate emissions and adaptation challenges.
    \item{SSP3 – Regional Rivalry:} This pathway depicts a fragmented world marked by regional conflicts and nationalism. Economic growth is slow and cooperation on climate issues is limited, leading to high emissions and significant adaptation challenges due to a lack of resources and technology transfer.
    \item{SSP4 – Inequality:} This scenario highlights a world characterized by stark inequalities, where socio-economic disparities hinder effective climate action. Wealthier regions are better able to adapt, while poorer regions remain highly vulnerable, resulting in high emissions and considerable difficulties in ensuring climate resilience.
    \item{SSP5 – Fossil-fueled Development:} This pathway envisions rapid economic growth driven by fossil fuel consumption. It assumes the absence of stringent climate policies, resulting in very high greenhouse gas emissions and severe climate impacts, with limited adaptive capacity.
\end{itemize}

Figure \ref{fig:climate_projections} graphically summarizes temperature increase projections from 2020 to 2100.
The top-right panel shows projections from five SSP for three ranges of Equilibrium Climate Sensitivity (ECS). Climate sensitivity considers the temperature change associated with a doubling of \ce{CO2} concentration in the atmosphere. At equilibrium, this sensitivity not only reflects the warming potential of greenhouse gases but also the feedback mechanisms within the climate system, including those related to atmospheric water vapour, clouds, and ice albedo. These estimates are based on general circulation models of the ocean and atmosphere.

The top-left panel shows the probability distribution of ECS levels. This distribution indicates that, for a doubling of atmospheric \ce{CO2} relative to pre-industrial levels (the conventional expression of ECS), the expected warming ranges between 2.5°C and 4°C.

The projections corresponding to this ECS range are shown in the right panel “Mid-range ECS.” It shows that the 1.5°C global warming limit, targeted by the Paris Agreement, will be exceeded during the 2030s under any SSP scenario. Depending on the scenario, the 2°C threshold (which the Paris Agreement aims to stay well below) will be crossed between 2040 and 2055 for the three least ambitious climate policy scenarios (SSP5, SSP3, SSP2) and will not be exceeded only under the two SSP1 scenarios.
The two lower panels illustrate two types of consequences of global warming for three levels of warming: 1.5°C, 2.0°C, and 4.0°C, across different world regions.
The first row relates to the Heat Warning Index. This index represents the number of days per year where temperature and humidity conditions reach dangerous levels for human health. Since the human body cools via sweating (with sweat evaporation helping to reduce body temperature), relative humidity plays a key role in tolerating high heat. For a given temperature, a lower humidity level makes the heat more bearable. The maximum tolerable temperature for a healthy young adult doing minimal physical activity is estimated at \SI{50}{\degreeCelsius} with \SI{10}{\%} relative humidity or \SI{35}{\degreeCelsius} with \SI{70}{\%} humidity.
The maps show, for instance, that at \SI{2}{\degreeCelsius} warming, regions within the intertropical zone would face a heat warning index of 30 to 180 days per year.
Linking this to the projections in the top part of the figure, and assuming an SSP2-4.5 scenario, these effects are expected around the year 2050. These regions were home to more than one-third of the global population in 2020, raising concerns about future habitability in large parts of the world.

The bottom row shows changes in extreme rainfall events. The values express the percentage increase in rainfall on the wettest day of the year. Climate warming leads to increased air humidity (according to the Clausius–Clapeyron relation, every \SI{1}{\degreeCelsius} increase means \SI{7}{\%} more relative humidity), resulting in more intense rainfall in a warmer atmosphere. There are, of course, regional and seasonal variations to this trend, but it generally holds. Local variations can also be linked to changes in atmospheric circulation. Regional feedbacks are also possible; for instance, the presence or absence of vegetation (linked to rainfall changes) can, in turn, influence rainfall levels.
By 2050, under the mid-range ECS, the least ambitious trajectories place us at warming levels between \num{2} and \SI{4}{\degreeCelsius}, while the most ambitious ones keep us between \num{1} and \SI{2}{\degreeCelsius}. It is clear that extreme heat and rainfall events remain much more contained below \SI{2}{\degreeCelsius} than at \SI{4}{\degreeCelsius}, entire regions of Africa or South America would face heat levels dangerous to human health for more than half the year.

\subsection{The silent collapse}
\label{sec:silent_collapse}
We did not mention it yet because it is not related to climate, but its importance is so immense that I cannot avoid dedicating a small section to it.
Apart from the influence of human activities in disturbing the planetary climate, the first and most striking consequence of modern global capitalism is the organised destruction of ecosystems.
The numbers are overwhelming: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Over \SI{75}{\percent} of flying insects populations have disappeared in protected areas over the last \SI{27}{years} \citep{hallmann_more_2017}.
    \item Same analysis for the arthropods\----the most diversified living group\----, over three quarters have gone in a few decades \citep{seibold_arthropod_2019}.%, birds populations are ebbing dramatically with 
    \item The global rate of extinction is estimated to be \SIrange{100}{1000}{times} greater than the background rate \citep{lamkin_challenge_2016}.
    \item The total disparition of living individuals, all species and groups combined, amounts to around \SI{70}{\percent} \citep{ipbes_summary_2019}.
    \item The living planet index\footnote{The living planet index (LPI) is a measure of the state of the world's biological diversity based on population trends of vertebrate species from terrestrial, freshwater and marine habitats.} has decreased of approximately \SI{20}{\percent} in North America and Europe since the 70's, but far from our eyes it has already collapsed: \SI{-55}{\percent} in South-east Asia and Oceania, \SI{-64}{\percent} in Africa and \SI{-94}{\percent} in South America.
\end{itemize}

The causes are known and understood. Almost all human development is achieved to the detriment of non-human living beings. In other words, human activities affect negatively \SI{99.9}{\percent} of the total biomass on Earth \citep{bar-on_biomass_2018}.
This appears clearly when looking at the distribution of mammal mass: \SI{60}{\percent} are livestock, \SI{34}{\percent} are humans, and \SI{4}{\percent} only are wild mammals. As for birds: \SI{70}{\percent} are chickens and other poultry, and only \SI{30}{\percent} are among wildlife \citep{bar-on_biomass_2018}.
Even though domestic animals now compose the greatest part of ground living vertebrates, their time on Earth is extremely short: just in France, \SI{3}{million} animals are killed every day. Worldwide, this number goes up to \SI{400}{millions} to reach 150 billion every year. This is without taking into account marine animals who represent \SI{96}{\percent} of killed animals for human supply\footnote{See the list of references from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) at \url{https://www.l214.com/animaux/statistiques-nombre-animaux-abattus-monde-viande//}.}.

\tquote{While more food, energy and

increasingly at the expense of nature’s ability
to provide such contributions in the future,
and frequently undermines nature’s many
other contributions, which range from water
quality regulation to sense of place. The

We may see less insects flying, although Europe is spared compared to the rest of the world. We may hear less birds singing, although birds represent only a tiny fraction of total living species.     \subsection{What are clouds}
    \label{sec:what_is_a_cloud}
    \tquote{Some say that black holes are the most complex objects in the Universe, although they're just regions of space-time from which the light cannot escape. On the other hand, a cloud is a region of the atmosphere where thermodynamic and dynamical conditions contribute to enabling the formation of liquid or solid, or both, water particles that manage to stay in suspension in the air during an adequately long time for an impressionist to paint them, until a stochastic cascade effect causes all this water to fall, unless it doesn't and stays in the sky preventing any astrophysicist from studying black holes.}{}

    The Earth's atmosphere contains at any moment, not less than $~10^{25}$ hydrometeors, or water based particles in suspension. The majority are liquid water droplets, and the rest is ice crystals \citep{cantrell_production_2005}.
    In parallel, the overall cloud coverage on the planet tends to be of 60-70 \%.
    In one hand, these numbers quantitatively describe the whole ensemble of clouds, but do not help to picture a single cloud.
    On the other hand, it is fairly easy to point out a cloud just by looking at the sky, although this approach is purely qualitative.
    Luke Howard (1772-1864), sometime considered as the \texit{"Godfather of clouds"} for the stratus-cumulus-cirrus classification he set up (still used nowadays), took inspiration in the artwork of cloud representation, and more precisely among the romantic painters such as William Turner and Caspar David Friedrich \citep{howard_lxiv_1803}. Yet, their paintings were rarely scientifically accurate. 
    An other romantic painter, John Constable (1776-1837), used Howard's classification and scientific observations of his own to accurately represent clouds, before that photography could immortalised the sky's tempers.
    Even though the bare eye approach is the most natural to the human observer, it most likely misses the majority of physical properties that make a cloud, a \textit{cloud}.

    The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and the American Meteorological Society (AMS), do not agree on the cloud definition.
    The first defines it as:
    \begin{displayquote}
        An aggregate of very small water droplets, ice crystals, or a mixture of both, with its base above the Earth’s surface, which is \textit{perceivable} from the observation location.
    \end{displayquote}

    The second gives a broader definition that includes non water clouds:
    \begin{displayquote}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item A visible aggregate of minute water droplets and/or ice particles in the atmosphere above the Earth’s surface.            
            \item Any collection of particulate matter in the atmosphere dense enough to be perceptible to the eye, as a dust cloud or smoke cloud \citep{world_meteorological_organisation_international_2018}.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{displayquote}

    The main distinction is the inclusion of non water particles aggregate in the definition of AMS, while WMO only consider water particles (droplets and ice crystals). It overlaps with the definition of aerosol particles (see \cref{sec:INP}) which is rather uncomfortable.
    The second, and more subtile distinction, is about the cloud detection.
    AMS highlight the necessity of cloud to be visible to the eye. Conversely, WMO does not mention the naked eye detection, but rather speaks of \textit{perceivable} aggregates. 
    This includes instrumental observations, that are sometimes the only way to detect clouds. Indeed, the development of instrumental techniques for cloud observation has led to the discovery of perfectly invisible but stable aggregates of hydrometeors that follows cloud dynamics and radiative properties \citep{lesigne_extensive_2024}. 
    Looking at clouds through instruments implies the use of sharp thresholds on physical variables. The set of parameters will then defer from a discipline to another. In radiation, the optical thickness will rule, while in cloud microphysics, the cloud particles characteristics (number and radius) and the liquid and solid water contents will be essential.

    In \citet{spankuch_what_2022}\----where the cloud definition is extensively discussed\----the authors try to define the modelled cloud like the following:
    \begin{displayquote}
        From a modelling point of view a cloud could be tentatively defined as follows: a hydro-thermodynamic nonequilibrium system of the coexisting macrophases of water in a turbulent humid air atmosphere including (i) the metastable states of water (supersaturated vapour, undercooled water), (ii) the different hydrometeoric forms of water condensates, and (iii) primarily emitted and secondarily formed atmospheric soles (aerosol, hydrosol) and plasmosols, which are involved in the phase transitions of atmospheric water by triggering the removal of metastable states of water into stable ones by heterogeneous nucleation.
    \end{displayquote}

    For the purpose of this thesis and the work that will follow in the next chapters, we will stick the study of the parameters above mentioned. Despite the great interest that represent cloud classification, dynamics, and the astounding beauty born of the mixing of both, our modelling perspective will prevent us to consider cloud by other means than liquid water content (LWC), ice water content (IWC), optical depth, particle number concentration and cloud fraction.

    \subsection{Cloud role in climate}
    \label{sec:cloud_role}
    As already mentioned, the clouds cover approximatively two thirds of the Earth's surface. Arising from the evaporation of water, they are more present above oceans than continents. Some marine areas, where low pressures are semi-permanent (North Pacific and Atlantic), show an average cloud cover of 90 \%.
    Clouds have two main roles in the climate system. 
    First, they are at the heart of the water cycle. They greatly participate to the water transportation, and the processes that govern their formation and evolution are therefore responsible for the majority of biomes repartitions.
    They also transport energy through the latent heat of water vapour. As water needs energy to evaporate, it stores some when in gas phase. The associated enthalpy of vaporisation variation, expressed in energy unit per unit of mass, is of \SI{2.3e6}{\joule\per\kilogram} for the water. 
    When the vapour condensate in the cloud droplets, it gives back this energy to the air which induces a local warming. From there, air convection can be induced, sometimes leading to more condensation and more energy release.
    Super cloud formations, like thunder storm clouds or cumulonimbus, can deploy \SI{20000}{GW} \citep{chalon_combien_2014}. This is a hundred times more than the sum of all active power plants in France. Thus, clouds have the potential for great transformations, and are indeed quasi systematically implied in extreme weather events.

    In parallel, clouds stand at the interface between downward and upward radiative fluxes. Their white colour reflects efficiently the incoming solar radiation (SW), which explains why some clouds cast dense shadows on the ground. On the other hand, the water vapour they contain, is a very efficient greenhouse gas. Consequently, clouds can be very effective at absorbing upward infrared fluxes (LW) coming from the surface.
    The balance of those two effects of clouds on the radiative fluxes is called the Cloud Radiative Effect (CRE). It depends on both environmental factors and cloud properties:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Cloud properties}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Optical thickness: controls how much short wave is scattered/absorbed and how opaque the cloud is for long wave.
            \item Cloud-top altitude: drives the cloud-top temperature which itself determines the upward short wave emissions. Higher clouds have stronger long wave warming (i.e. greenhouse effect).
            \item Cloud phase: liquid droplets and ice crystals have different optical properties (scattering and emissivity), and thus affect differently the CRE.
            \item Cloud particles effective radius: at constant water content, cloud particles radius determine the number of particles. short wave reflectivity is proportional to particles number.
        \end{itemize}

        \item \textbf{Environmental factors}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Aerosols particles: impact cloud formation, lifetime, precipitation, particles number and phase (see \cref{sec:cloud_formation} and \cref{sec:aerosol_effects}).
            \item Surface albedo: over bright surfaces, clouds will not increase much the apparent albedo, leading to lower short wave effect.
            \item Time of day: during the night, clouds have by definition no short wave effect, only long wave. This is particularly important in polar regions where daytime highly varies.
            \item Solar zenith angle: cloud apparent thickness varies with the incident angle of short wave radiations.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}

    The CRE is not necessarily in balance for a singular cloud. On the contrary, it is most of the time negative at the surface. In other words, in spatial and temporal average, the clouds cool the surface. This is true at all latitudes except in polar regions where the clouds CRE at the surface is positive (see \cref{fig:matus_CRE}).

    \subsection{Cloud formation and evolution}
    \label{sec:cloud_formation}
    Clouds are among those physical systems that involve a wide range of disciplines. Cloud physics is a subtle combination of thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, radiative physics, and molecular physics.
    In this section, we will explore some of the main mechanisms that permit the existence of clouds in the Earth's atmosphere.

    \subsubsection{Notions of stability}
    In order to meet the favorable conditions of cloud formation, an air parcel has to be subject to some dynamical movement.

    The atmosphere\----or more precisely, a portion or layer of it\----can be classified into three different stability states. These characterize whether the considered atmospheric layer is convective, stable, or neutral.
    
    The stability of an air parcel depends on the vertical balance between its weight and the buoyancy force:

    \begin{equation}
        m_p \vec{a} = \vec{\Pi} + \vec{P_p}
    \end{equation}

    where $ m_p $ is the mass of the air parcel, \( \vec{a} \) its acceleration, \( \vec{\Pi} \) the buoyancy force, and \( \vec{P_p} \) the weight of the parcel. The buoyancy \(\Pi\) depends on the density \(\rho_e\) of the surrounding air and the density of the parcel \(\rho_p\).
    
    The equation of state for air, considered as an ideal gas, is

    \begin{equation}
        P = \rho R_a T
    \end{equation}

    where \( R_a \) is the gas constant for dry air, equal to \SI{287}{\meter\square\per\second\square\per\kilogram}.
    Thus, 
    \begin{equation}
        \rho = \frac{P}{R_a T}.
    \end{equation}

    Since the pressure of the parcel and its surroundings are identical, it is the temperature difference between $T_p$ and $T_e$ that determines the ratio $\frac{\rho_p}{\rho_e}$ and, therefore, the stability state of the atmosphere.
    
    Consider an atmosphere initially in hydrostatic equilibrium (balance between pressure gradient force and gravity):

    \begin{equation}
        -\frac{1}{\rho_e} \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} = g
        \label{eq:hydrostatic}
    \end{equation}

    A change in density from \(\rho_e \to \rho_p\) induces a vertical acceleration \(a\) of the air parcel (now with density \(\rho_p\) different from the environment):
    \begin{equation}
        -\frac{1}{\rho_p} \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} = g + a
    \end{equation}

    Substituting \cref{eq:hydrostatic} into this expression, and using the equation of state, yields:

    \begin{equation}
    a = -g \frac{T_e - T_p}{T_e}
    \label{eq:acceleration}
    \end{equation}
    
    Equation~\eqref{eq:acceleration} can be rewritten as a differential equation by assuming linear temperature profiles of the form

    \begin{equation}
    T_i = T_0 + z\,\Gamma_i \quad\quad (i = e, p)
    \end{equation}

    and introducing the vertical derivative of potential temperature:

    \begin{equation}
    \frac{d^2 z}{dt^2} = - \frac{g}{T_0} \frac{d\theta}{dz} z
    \end{equation}

    The term $\frac{g}{T_0} \frac{d\theta}{dz}$ is the squared Brunt–Väisälä frequency\footnote{Actually, $N$ has the dimension of a pulsation.}:
   
   \begin{equation}
    N^2 = \frac{g}{T_0} \frac{d\theta}{dz}
    \end{equation}
    
    The Brunt–Väisälä frequency characterizes the oscillations of a fluid parcel that has been displaced, by any perturbation, from its hydrostatic equilibrium position. When the balance between weight and buoyancy is disturbed, these forces act to restore the parcel to equilibrium. Like a spring given an impulse, the parcel will oscillate at frequency \(N\) around its equilibrium position, if the oscillations are maintained. Such oscillations occur both in the atmosphere and in the ocean, where they are known as \emph{gravity waves}.
    
    In practice, the stability of an atmospheric layer can be determined by examining the vertical profile of potential temperature \(\theta\):
    \begin{itemize}
    \item The atmosphere is \textbf{stable} when \( N^2 > 0 \), corresponding to a positive vertical gradient of potential temperature.
    \item It is \textbf{unstable} when \( N^2 < 0 \), corresponding to a negative gradient of potential temperature.
    \end{itemize}

    
    %\subsubsection*{Qualitative interpretation}
    It is often convenient to visualize stability as follows:
    
    \paragraph{Stable case:}  
    When the vertical temperature gradient of the parcel is steeper (i.e., more negative) than that of the environment (\(\nabla_z T_p < \nabla_z T_e\), with \(\nabla_z T_e = -g / c_p\)), the atmosphere is stable. If the parcel is given an upward motion (e.g., by wind advection), buoyancy acts as a restoring force, returning it to its equilibrium altitude. This situation is analogous to a mechanical potential well associated with stable equilibrium.
    
    \paragraph{Unstable case:}  
    When the vertical temperature gradient of the parcel is weaker (less negative) than that of the environment (\(\nabla_z T_p > \nabla_z T_e\)), the atmosphere is unstable. In this case, if the parcel is given kinetic energy that displaces it from equilibrium, buoyancy amplifies its upward motion. This is analogous to a mechanical potential peak associated with unstable equilibrium.
    
    \paragraph{Neutral case:}  
    When the gradients are equal (\(\nabla_z T_p = \nabla_z T_e\)), the atmosphere is neutral. Once the parcel’s kinetic energy is dissipated, buoyancy and gravity balance, keeping the parcel at its new altitude.

        \begin{figure}[h]
            \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{./chap1/images/stability.png}
            \caption{Atmospheric stability by temperature gradient (B), and associated mechanical analogy (A).}
            \label{fig:stability}
        \end{figure}

    For cloud formation, the air parcel has to be unstable. This situation allows the air to be lifted; the phenomenon is called \textit{free convection}. Conversely, \textit{forced convection} refers to upward air displacement caused by pressure gradient or topography.
    With the troposphere temperature gradient, the lifted parcel might meet thermodynamically favorable conditions for its vapour to condensate. 
    For low tropospheric clouds like cumulus (see \cref{fig:cloud_pic} XX), the sine qua none condition for droplet forming is often on temperature. When the isotherm that allows the cloud to form is reached, the vapour condensates and the cloud forms. That explains why those kind of clouds have very flat and homogeneous bases.
    The air parcel can eventually reach an altitude where $N^2 > 0$, and thus stop its ascension. 
    If the parcel is in a stable case, the cloud can be the witness of gravity waves (see \cref{fig:cloud_pic}).
     
    However, when the droplets form, the latent heat associated to their initial evaporation is released, which warms the surrounding air. This tends to fuel the air parcel lifting. If the total latent heat of the parcel is sufficiently important, and the negative potential temperature gradient is strong enough, the lifting can enter in a positive feedback loop that causes it to self sustain until all the vapour has condensed. We talk of \textit{deep convection}.
    In such situations, the cloud can extend up to the tropopause where the tropospheric temperature gradient gets cancelled, and so do the buoyancy of the air parcel. 
    This mechanism is at the origin of cumulonimbus formation.


    \subsubsection{Cloud phase}
    \label{sec:cloud_phase}
    On Earth, tropospheric clouds are composed of water, which is present under its three phases in the atmosphere: liquid, solid, and gaseous.
    Clouds can be classified as liquid-phase clouds (LPCs), solid-phase clouds (SPCs), or mixed-phase clouds (MPCs), in which supercooled water droplets and ice crystals coexist. 
    
    When the formation of one or the other of these phases occurs without the intervention of a solid surface or particles, it is referred to as \textit{homogeneous nucleation} (c.f. \cref{sec:ice_nucleation}). The formation of water droplets by homogeneous nucleation can only occur at supersaturation levels of several hundred percent (never encountered on Earth). Likewise, only below $-38$°C can ice crystals form through a homogeneous process \citep{seinfeld_atmospheric_1998}. Homogeneous solid-phase nucleation, or \textit{ice nucleation}, is observed almost exclusively during the formation of cirrus clouds (high tropospheric clouds).
    
    It is because their formation is facilitated by the presence of aerosols\----liquid or solid particles suspended in the air (c.f. \cref{sec:INP})\----that clouds still cover more than two-thirds of the globe’s surface. 
    When aerosol particles help the formation of cloud particles, the process is called \textit{heterogeneous nucleation}. This process drastically lowers the humidity level required for liquid-phase formation, and increases the temperature at which ice crystals form; as a result, either phase can develop from small supersaturations, and ice crystal formation can begin at temperatures as high as 0°C.

    \subsubsection{Precipitations}
    The clouds are known to be at the origin of rain and snow, but not all clouds precipitate. Some clouds do not precipitate, like cumulus humilis and stratus, whilst others are intrinsically precipitating clouds, like nimbus, the \textit{rain clouds}. Cirrus are also precipitating clouds, but their icy precipitations never reach the ground. Convective cells, that born as cumulus and might end up as cumulonimbus, can precipitate both liquid water and agglomeration of ice crystals under the form of hail or graupel.

    \subsection{Physics of ice nucleation}
    \label{sec:ice_nucleation}
%    Citation from Bernard Brunhes, \textit{La dégradation de l'énergie}, page ???.

    We saw that ice nucleation can happen in a pristine atmosphere through what is called homogeneous nucleation, or at higher temperatures with the help of aerosols particles through heterogeneous nucleation.
    Once cloud temperature falls below \SI{0}{\degreeCelsius}, ice crystals can form within it. This process, called ice nucleation, denotes both the phase transition of vapour to ice, and the freezing of a liquid droplets.
    Unlike the formation of cloud droplets, which never occur homogeneously (i.e. without the help of an aerosol particle) in the atmosphere, the freezing of cloud particles can happen by both homogeneous and heterogeneous nucleations.
    Since we focus on aerosol-cloud interactions (ACI) in this thesis, we are particularly interested in the second process. Furthermore, the fact that ice crystals can form homogeneously and heterogeneously gives to ice nucleation an additional dimension: aerosols particles that can nucleate ice have the ability to completely change a cloud life story. This is why heterogeneous ice nucleation is particularly interesting and crucial when modelling clouds; uncertainties on heterogeneous ice nucleation are at the roots of cloud\----and by extension climate\----modelling uncertainties.

    \subsubsection{The energy barrier}
    \label{sec:energy_barrier}
    % --> energy barrier
    %see chapter 8 and 9 of K and Curry. And chapter 8 of Lhomann for simplicity (p. 226)
    In order to understand the physics behind heterogeneous nucleation, it is necessary to recall how homogeneous nucleation works. The following are the basics of classical nucleation theory (CNT).
    What prevents the spontaneous formation of an ice crystal from water vapour of a liquid droplet, is the competition between two energy terms: a volume term favorable to the phase transition, and a surface term that resists the nucleation.
       \begin{equation}
           \Delta G = \Delta G_{\text{vol}} + \Delta G_{\text{surf}}
       \end{equation}

    Let us assume the formation of a spherical ice embryo of radius $r$.
    The volume term comes from the released latent heat of the phase transition, which lowers the Gibbs free energy of the system. It scales with the volume of the ice embryo:
       \begin{equation}
           \Delta G_{\text{vol}} = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \Delta g_{\text{v}}
       \end{equation}
       $\Delta g_{\text{vol}}$ is the volumetric Gibbs free-energy change associated with the phase transition, and expressed in \si{J m^{-3}}.

    The surface term expresses the energy cost due to the creation of an ice-water or ice-vapour interface. It is due to the molecules unsatisfied bonds at the phases interface, and is the origin of surface tension. The term scales with the surface area:
       \begin{equation}
           \Delta G_{\text{surf}} = 4 \pi r^2 \sigma_i
       \end{equation}
       where $\sigma_i$ is the surface tension between the new phase and the parent phase: $\sigma_{i,v}$ for vapour to ice, and $\sigma_{i,l}$ for liquid to ice, both assumed to be anisotropic.

       The Gibbs free energy change of nucleation thus express as:
       \begin{equation}
           \label{eq:G}
           \Delta G(r) = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \Delta g_{v} + 4 \pi r^2 \sigma_i
       \end{equation}

       Surface term $\propto r^2$ opposes formation; volume term $\propto r^3$ favours growth when $|\Delta g_v}|$ is large enough. The maximum of $\Delta G(r)$ is the nucleation energy barrier $\Delta G_c$ at critical radius $r_c$. They are derived for $\frac{\partial \Delta G(r)}{\partial r} = 0$, which lead to the critical radius expression:
       \begin{equation}
           r_c = - \frac{2\sigma_\_i{\Delta g_{v}} 
            \label{eq:r_c}
       \end{equation}
       Injected in the expression of $\Delta G(r)$, we get:
       \begin{equation}
           \Delta G_c = \frac{16}{3} \pi \frac{\sigma_i^3}{|\Delta g_v|^2}
           \label{eq:G_c}
       \end{equation}
       where $\Delta G_c$ is in \si{J}, $\sigma_i$ in \si{J m^{-2}}, $\Delta g_{v}}$ in \si{J m^{-3}} and $r_c$ in \si{m}.


       The nucleation rate is usually written as follows,
       \begin{equation}
           J = J_0 exp\left(-\frac{\Delta G_c}{k_B T}\right)
       \end{equation}
       expressed in \si{\per\second} and where $J_0$ is a kinetic prefactor, and $k_B$ the Boltzmann's constant.

       The expression of $\Delta g_{v}$ varies whether the phase transition is vapour to ice, or liquid to ice:
       \paragraph{Vapour to ice:}
       In this case, the driving force for the ice embryo formation is the vapour supersaturation relative to ice:
       \begin{equation}
           S_i = \frac{p_{\text{vapour}}}{p_{\text{sat,ice}}(T)}
       \end{equation}
       
       The temperature and $k_B$ intervene, along with $v_{\text{molecule}}$ the molecular volume of ice, to express $\Delta g_{v}$ as a volumique energy density:
       \begin{equation}
           \Delta g_{v,v} = - \frac{k_B T}{v_{\text{molecule}}} ln S_i 
       \end{equation}
       When the vapour is supersaturated ($S_i>1$), $\Delta g_{v,v} < 0$ which favours solid growth in \cref{eq:G}. Higher is $S_i$, lower gets the critical radius $r_c$ (\cref{eq:r_c}) and the energy barrier $\Delta G_c$ (\cref{eq:G_c}).

       \paragraph{Liquid to ice:}
       Here, the driving force for phase transition is the supercooling $\Delta T = T_m-T$.
       The latent heat released during freezing of liquid water $L_f$ and the ice density $\rho_i$ have to be taken into account in order to express $\Delta g_{v,l}$:
       \begin{equation}
           \Delta g_{v,l} \approx - \rho_i L_f\, ln \frac{T_m}{T}
       \end{equation}
       where we make the approximation that $L_f \approx \Delta h$, the enthalpy of changing state.
       When the supercooling is positive (i.e. the temperature is under the melting point of water $T_m$), $T_m > T$ which implies $\Delta g_v < 0$. This create favorable condition for ice growth and lowers the critical radius and the energy barrier.
       \vspace{\baselineskip}

       In the case of heterogeneous nucleation, the energy barrier is reduced by the presence of what is called an ice nucleating particle (INP). CNT describes the effect of INPs through the contact angle $\theta$ between it and the ice embryo (\cref{fig:hetero_nucleation} A). Irregularities called \textit{active sites} on the INP surface, such as bumps or crevasses, help the accumulation of water molecules for the formation of an ice embryo. Consequently, $\Delta G_c$ is lowered by a factor $f$ that depends, in CNT, on the contact angle:
       \begin{equation}
           \Delta G_{c,het} = f(\theta)\,\Delta G_{c,hom} 
       \end{equation}
        The presence of an INP can reduce so much the energy barrier, that heterogeneous nucleation can happen at much warmer temperature than homogeneous nucleation (\cref{fig:hetero_nucleation} B).

            \begin{figure}[h]
                \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{./chap1/images/hetero_nucleation.png}
                \caption{Heterogeneous ice nucleation. Panel A is a schematic of the formation of an ice embryo in contact of an INP. The contact angle $\theta$ and the surface tensions $\sigma_i$ of the different actors are represented (adapted from \citet{lohmann_introduction_2016}). Panel B is the energy barrier $\Delta  G$ expressed in thermal units, function of the ice embryo radius, for homogeneous and heterogeneous ice nucleation. The curves maximum is the critical Gibbs free energy that, once reached allows the embryo to grow as an ice crystal.}
                \label{fig:hetero_nucleation}
            \end{figure}

       % why the threshold of -38°C
        \subsubsection*{Note about the \SI{-38}{\degreeCelsius} threshold}
        It is common to consider the temperature condition for homogeneous nucleation as a threshold in the vicinity of $-38$°C, but the physical origin for that threshold is rarely explained nor even mentioned. However, the elements of CNT developed herein above can allow us to understand it.
        Figure \ref{fig:nuc_rate} shows the ice nucleation rate for homogeneous freezing from liquid droplet to ice (green line). One can see that it is extremely low at temperatures above $-40$°C, and can be considered as zero below $30$°C.
        On the other hand, it gets to values that could allow actual formation of ice crystal below $-40$°C, and even high rates below $-50$°C.
        The blue line depicts the average mean time of nucleation. It is even more striking that above $-40$°C, the homogeneous nucleation can not happen. At $-35$°C, it takes \SI{1e18}{s} for a droplet to freeze, or approximatively $32$ billion years. However, at \SI{-50}{\degreeCelsius}, it only takes \SI{10}{s} in average.        

        In conclusion, this threshold of $-38$°C is half quantitative\--half qualitative. It marks the beginning of acceptable and rapidly increasing values of nucleation rate, but still very few ice crystal can form homogeneously at this temperature.
        Therefore, INPs active at temperature around the homogeneous temperature threshold (see \cref{sec:INP_types}) can still have a significant impact on the solid phase of cloud.

            \begin{figure}[h]
                \centering
                \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{./chap1/images/homogeneous_nucleation_rate.png}
                \caption{Representation of theoretical droplet freezing rate by homogeneous nucleation (liquid to ice) versus temperature. The corresponding average time needed for an ice crystal to form is plotted on the second axis. In the vicinity of $-38$°C, the nucleation rate rapidly decreases with increasing temperature, while the average time before freezing dramatically increases.}
                \label{fig:nuc_rate}
            \end{figure}

    \paragraph{Nota bene:} CNT is a powerful tool to understand the physics of nucleation. However, and even though its tentative in describing heterogeneous nucleation is very physical, it remains a poor description of the complexity of aerosol-cloud interactions happening in a nucleation event. \citet{fletcher_active_1969} have shown that INPs can have complex structures of active sites that can greatly influence the nucleation.
Many recent studies have tried to take this \texit{active sites} approach into account \citep[e.g.]{murray_ice_2012, niemand_particle-surface-area-based_2012, kanji_heterogeneous_2013, hummel_simulating_2018}. 
In \cref{chap4}, more evidences will arise about the short coming of CNT for the modelling of ice nucleation.

    \subsubsection{Types of heterogeneous nucleation}
        Heterogeneous ice nucleation can happen through four different processes.

        \paragraph{Immersion freezing:}
        Immersion freezing refers to the formation of an ice crystal from a droplet inside which an INP is immersed. It can occur at $S_i = 1$ and $T \leq 0~^{\circ}$C, but requires the preliminary formation of a liquid droplet and thus passing through a sufficiently high saturation ratio for liquid condensation to have taken place.
        
        \paragraph{Deposition nucleation:}
        Along with immersion freezing, deposition nucleation is the dominant process in the formation of atmospheric clouds. Water vapour can sometimes deposit directly on the surface of INPs. When the humidity conditions ($S_i = 1$) and temperature ($T \leq 0~^{\circ}$C) allow, an ice crystal can form from the INP on which water molecules have deposited.
        
        \paragraph{Condensation freezing:}
        When air approaches saturation, liquid water can condense on the surface of INPs without forming a droplet. If saturation is reached but remains below the threshold for droplet formation, an ice crystal can form. In general, INPs contain soluble components that can eventually act as CCN. If condensation freezing does not occur quickly enough, a droplet may form on the INP. In this case, immersion freezing will occur if ice crystal formation takes place.
        
        \paragraph{Contact freezing:}
        The collision of a supercooled water droplet with an INP can lead to the formation of an ice crystal if supersaturation conditions are met ($S_i = 1$ and $T \leq 0~^{\circ}$C). Contact freezing is limited by the collision rate between droplets and INPs, which depends on the INP concentration as well as the size distribution of the particles and droplets.

    Aerosol particles, independently of their nucleation ability, can be defined as non-water liquid or solid particles in suspension in the air.

As mentioned before, this thesis focuses on the processes related to cloud ice formation, consequently, this section will investigate the nature and properties of INPs.

    \subsection{The variety of aerosols effects on cloud and climate}
    \label{sec:aerosol_effects}
    Before getting to the particularities of INPs, let us take a glimpse to the variety of aerosols effects on clouds and climate. 

     The first effect of aerosols is what is called the \textit{direct effect}. It is related to the optical properties of aerosols particles. Indeed, they can reflect, absorb, scatter or transmit incident short wave radiations, which impact the radiative balance of the atmosphere. 
     Beyond the direct effect, exist two other types of impact of these particles on the climate energy budget. Those concern the particles interactions with clouds (Fig.~\ref{fig:aerosol_effects}).

    \paragraph{Semi-direct effects:}
    The presence of an aerosol plume, through its interactions with solar radiation, causes a warming of the air layer it occupies. This modification of the vertical temperature profile of the atmosphere induces, in cases where temperature decreases with altitude, an increase in hydrostatic stability. This phenomenon can inhibit atmospheric convection and consequently alter cloud formation by reducing their vertical development in favour of horizontal spreading. This leads to an increase in stratus cloud coverage and a reduction in cumulus development. In the Arctic, due to the low solar exposure associated with high latitudes, the direct and semi-direct effects of aerosols are not very pronounced. Indirect effects have the most significant impact on the region’s energy budget.
    
    \paragraph{Indirect effects:}
    Indirect effects are the consequences of aerosol–cloud interactions. While these interactions can be very complex, the general sequence of mechanisms is straightforward to understand. An increase in CCN concentration will promote the formation of a larger number of droplets, which in turn increases the cloud’s optical thickness and infrared emissivity \citep{twomey_influence_1977}. This is referred to as the first indirect effect. Furthermore, for a constant liquid water content, an increase in droplet number leads to a decrease in their mean size. The coalescence processes responsible for droplet growth will then be less efficient, delaying cloud precipitation. This is the second indirect effect, which results in an increased cloud lifetime \citep{albrecht_cloud_1989}.

            \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{./chap1/images/aerosols_effects.jpg}
            \caption{Schematics of the different main aerosols effects on clouds and the radiative budget. Graphics from \citet{ipcc_AR4_2007}.}
            \label{fig:aerosol_effects}
        \end{figure}
    
    Many other indirect effects exist, such as the impact of aerosols on cloud altitude or on ice clouds. These processes, however, remain poorly understood, mainly due to gaps in our understanding of aerosol–cloud interactions.

    \subsection{Physical requirements of INPs}
     For an aerosol particle to be able to act as an INP, its physical properties have to meet specific requirements.
     Because of the way heterogeneous ice nucleation works, i.e. the formation of a molecule cluster on active sites\----which helps the ice embryo to reach the critical size of nucleation\----, good INPs are rather insoluble particles. 
     For the same reason, INPs size is a critical factor for their efficiency. Indeed, the number of active sites is proportional to the particle surface. Its type (mineral, carbon organic or inorganic) will partly determine the active sites density of the INP.
     Eventually, its molecular bindings with water (i.e. is it hydrophilic or hydrophobic) can impact its nucleation abilities.

     The INPs are often distinguished by their activation temperature, i.e. the temperature at which they form ice crystals. In the following, we will call warm INPs particles that nucleate ice at temperatures higher than \tempc{-15}, and we will talk about cold INPs when the activation temperature is lower this arbitrary threshold.
     In this thesis, we will encounter very warm INPs that can nucleate ice up to \tempc{-3}, but we will rarely mention very cold INPs (efficient at temperatures around the homogeneous nucleation threshold) like soots.

    \subsection{Natural or anthropogenic? The diversity of INPs}
    \label{sec:INP_types}

    !!!TODO!!!  enrich and add references and address anthropogenic INPs
    
    Arctic INPs are poorly characterized, but the current state of research suggests that they are mainly primary aerosols: mineral dust and particles of biological, marine, or terrestrial origin \citep{hartmann_terrestrial_2021}. For each of these INP types, several impacts of climate warming on their emissions\----and therefore on their concentrations in the Arctic atmosphere\----can be identified.
    
    \subsubsection{Sea salts and marine organics}
    Sea salts are water-soluble in composition, which makes them poor INPs. However, they play a major role as CCN. Moreover, they share their emission mechanism with marine organic particles, which are very efficient INPs. These two aerosol types are grouped under the term sea sprays, which refers to their emission mechanism.
    
    These aerosols are emitted during the bursting of fine water droplets projected at the ocean surface (illustrated on Fig.~\ref{fig:seasprays_pic}). The lifting of foam by swell and wind is the main production mechanism. Thus, changes in surface winds in oceanic regions influence INP concentrations, but it is primarily the variation in the ocean surface in contact with the atmosphere (open ocean) that determines the emission of marine aerosols. This is particularly relevant in polar regions, especially in the Arctic, where the open ocean surface changes with both the seasonal cycle of sea ice and its multi-decadal decline associated with climate warming (see Fig.~I.9). This link between sea ice and the emission of sea salts and marine organics suggests that the ongoing decrease in sea ice concentration—seemingly inevitable given current warming trajectories—will be accompanied by an increase in the concentration of this aerosol type.
        \begin{figure}[h]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{./chap1/images/nuages/seasprays_pic_HD.jpg}
            \caption{Sea sprays are emitted during the bursting of fine water droplets projected by wind and waves. They contain sea salts and organic matter from marine life.}
            \label{fig:seasprays_pic}
        \end{figure}
    
    \subsubsection{Biological sources, terrestrial and marine}
    Particles produced by biological activity can act as INPs when emitted into the atmosphere, and in some cases—such as bacteria—can be particularly active at temperatures above $-15$°C \citep{storelvmo_aerosol_2017}. Pollen, plant debris, bacteria, and viruses are the main representatives of continental organic aerosols. Marine organic INPs consist of residues from biological activity, such as algal, planktonic, or phytoplankton debris.
    
    \citet{creamean_ice_2019} show that warming of the Arctic Ocean and the retreat of sea ice cover are two factors that promote biological activity at high northern latitudes. Similarly, \citet{creamean_thawing_2020} describe how permafrost thaw exposes the atmosphere to important INP sources through the re-emergence of biological organisms, once again exposed to the atmosphere. In general, temperature acts—up to a certain point—as a catalyst for microbial activity. Climate warming is therefore expected to mechanically increase the presence of biological INPs in cold regions. For example, it drives the migration of marine biological activity toward higher latitudes at a rate of about 1° per decade \citep{renaut_northward_2018}. All of these temperature-driven changes could lead to an increase in biological INP concentrations in the Arctic.
    
    \subsubsection{Mineral dust}
    Among aerosols capable of acting as INPs, mineral dust is often considered as the most common, and consequently as the most important. This relies on their large size (which confer them high amount of active sites) and on the high emissions fluxes of such particles in the atmosphere.    
    Mineral dust is most often lofted by wind or projected into the air through the impact of other particles (saltation and sandblasting processes). They originate mainly from semi-arid regions or deserts but can also be emitted from riverbeds or glaciers. Again, climate warming is expected to increase the number of sites favorable to the emission of these particles through glacier melt and riverbed drying. In areas where warming leads to a retreat of arid zones due to increasing biodiversity, the decrease in mineral INPs could be partially offset by the rise in biological sources.
 Although they are active at lower temperature than biological INPs, they can participate to transport the latter and thus extend their range of action. However, due to their relatively short atmospheric lifetime—linked in part to their high density\----they can also limit the transport of biological INPs.

    \subsection{Lifetime and deposition processes}
    \label{sec:depostition_processes}
    !!!TODO!!!  add references

    Unlike some stable chemical species such as \ce{CO2}, aerosols have a short lifetime in the atmosphere. Several processes contribute to the decrease in atmospheric aerosol concentrations, so that their residence time in the troposphere ranges from a few hours to about ten days. These processes can be classified into three types: dynamical, chemical, and microphysical.
    
    In the absence of any interaction with other chemical elements, the lifetime of aerosols in the atmosphere is limited by dynamical processes. The main ones are the mechanisms of dry and wet deposition. Dry deposition refers to the sedimentation of particles under the effect of gravity. It depends on the balance between the weight of the aerosols and the various resistances to this sedimentation.
    
    However, dry deposition accounts for only \SIrange{10}{20}{\percent} of aerosol removal from the atmosphere; wet deposition is the dominant dynamical process. Near emission sources, on the other hand, various dry deposition processes dominate, particularly those affecting larger particles. One example is impaction: when the inertia due to the particle's mass ejects it from the trajectory of the air parcel carrying it, possibly leading to its collision with the surface. Similarly, interception refers to the collision of the particle with the surface due to its size when its trajectory passes too close to it. Smaller particles are more affected by Brownian motion, which is the dominant process in their dry deposition.
    
    The term wet deposition refers to the removal of aerosols from the atmosphere by precipitation. This can occur in different ways. Aerosols can be carried to the ground by falling precipitation (rain or snow) when they are located below or within a cloud: this is the \emph{washout} process. Alternatively, after serving as cloud condensation nuclei or ice nuclei, aerosols are trapped in droplets or ice crystals and are brought to the ground during the precipitation of the cloud particles they helped forming. This is referred to as \emph{rainout}.
    
    As will be seen in the next chapters, these processes must be taken into account when attempting to simulate the transport of aerosols in order to trace them back to their emission sources (\cref{chap3}), and when simulating aerosols particles emission and transport (\cref{chap4}).

    \subsection{Seasonal variability and observational ability}
    \label{sec:INP_seasonality}
    Like many variables and atmospheric species, INPs can present a seasonal cycle that could eventually be detected in the observed concentrations. 
    Theoretical explanations for this variability appears to be as a matter of course. Emissions can be subject to weather conditions (wind, snow cover, temperature), to vegetation cycle (for continental organic INPs) or to sea ice cover (for oceanic emissions). Atmospheric transport of particles is also subject to synoptic weather configurations that vary along the year, which can affect the local concentrations of remote sourced species. Furthermore, the wind and precipitations can have a strong impact on particles removal (c.f. \cref{sec:depostition_processes}).

    Figure \ref{fig:Ninp_Wex} illustrates how much can vary INP concentrations across the seasons.
    Panel A shows the concentrations for five activation temperatures, ranging from \SI{-10}{\degreeCelsius} to \SI{-18}{\degreeCelsius}, of INP measurements performed at Utqiaġvik (Alaska) over the year 2012-2013 \citep{wex_annual_2019}. 
    The \SI{-10}{\degreeCelsius} series, or concentrations of the warmest INPs, is the one that shows the highest variability. It peaks during summer and spring, around $10^{-2}$ \si{INP\per\liter}, its lowest values are seen during winter down to three orders of magnitude lower.
    Comparison with panel B, that shows the Arctic average temperature and precipitation, suggests a causal relationship between the climate seasonal cycle and the observed variability in INP measurements.
    Without further analysis, the evidence remains light, but the theoretical background mentioned above, still is plausible. 
    However, this plot suggests an other explanation for this apparent variability.

    % --> figure of Wex measurements
        \begin{figure}[h]
            \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{./chap1/images/Ninp_cycle_Utqiagvik.png}
            \caption{One year (2012-2013) time series of INP concentrations active at different temperatures. Panel A shows the raw concentrations alongside polynomial fits. The magnitude of the seasonal cycle varies with the activation temperature. Panel B is the averaged Arctic temperature and precipitation on the same period. Data from \citet{wex_annual_2019}.}
            \label{fig:Ninp_Wex}
        \end{figure}

    The concentrations for the different activation temperatures show seasonal variability that weaken continuously with the temperature. While the stronger variability is met for the INP active at $-10$°C, concentrations are almost stable at $-18$°C. 
    Whilst this could be explained by the variability inherent to the different species implied on this INP temperature spectrum, the continuous weakening of the variability, and the fact it happens in winter, suggest something else. 
    Warmer INPs are much more easily activated in clouds during winter when the temperature are lower, while the colder INPs are not as much activated. Thus, the natural in-cloud activation of the warmest INPs would prevent them to get captured by the air inlet of the aerosol filtering instruments.
    In the meantime, colder INPs would remain inactivated rather because of temperature condition, or because the solid phase would have already been formed on the warm INPs.
    This would lead to a systematic bias on the measurement of INPs, the warmest being under-represented and presenting a strong apparent seasonal cycle compared to the steadier cold INP concentrations.

    During the one year Arctic campaign MOSAIC (see \cref{sec:observation_data}), \citet{creamean_annual_2022} observe the same variability on INP concentrations: high concentrations during summer and spring, and low to zero warm INPs during winter, while the cold INPs have an almost constant concentration level all the year.
    It questions the reliability of INP measurement techniques, as well as the conclusions drawn from them. Although the theory behind the natural variability of INP concentrations is solid, it has to be investigated with different techniques than just observation.
    In this context, modelling appears to be a powerful tool to test conceptual understandings, to remove doubt and confirm hypothesis.
    Chapter~\ref{chap4} will investigate this question by addressing the problem of INP modelling.

The name Arctic finds its roots in ancient Greek: ἄρκτος [árktos], which means bear. Despite the fact the Arctic is commonly known as the place of polar bears, antiquity Greeks did not know it back then. The exploration of the Arctic region by Europeans is much more recent with many exploratory expeditions in the 19\up{th} century. As for the geographical North pole, it has been officially reached only during the advent of flying machines in the 20\up{th} century. Theses difficulties in Arctic exploration give evidence for how extreme is its climate, which still make modern observations difficult to carry.
Thus, the Greeks were rather referring to the constellations: the Great and the Little bears which embody the north in the sky\footnote{The Antarctic is therefore the anti-bear place, which is twice as true since it is located around the South pole, and because there is no bear there.}.

The Arctic region could be defined with different geographic thresholds. An astronomical threshold is used to set the latitude of the Arctic Circle, located at 66.5°N. Northward of this line, the current Earth obliquity causes the Sun not to rise on the day of the winter solstice; on the summer solstice, it does not set.
The July two meters temperature isotherm, is sometimes given as a more meteorological and dynamic definition. Indeed, with the global warming going on (c.f. \cref{sec:evidence_of_GW} and \cref{sec:polar_amp}), the Arctic is condemned to shrink. 
Similarly, the boreal tree line can be used as the Arctic border, but again, with the climate warming, this definition leads the Arctic into shrinking, although in this case, the Arctic Circle will always remains a backup.
Figure \ref{fig:arctic_map} gives an insight of the Arctic geography, and illustrates the above definitions.

    \subsection{A glimpse on Arctic climate conditions}
    \label{sec:arctic_glimpse} 
    As mentioned herein above, the Arctic is by definition a region where the weather is cold and the diurnal cycle very evolutive along the year. This has several implication for the Arctic atmosphere and radiative budget.
    % polar night
    The so-called \textit{polar night} gives a strong positive surface radiative forcing to clouds. Indeed, because there is no short wave radiations, remains only the greenhouse effect of clouds. Blocking infrared radiations, they strongly warm the surface.
    The low solar exposure and cold temperature, also causes a strong stability of the atmospheric boundary layer. This leads to very low convection and to keep atmospheric pollutants close to the surface.

    % ocean and sea ice
    Unlike Antarctica which is a continent, the Arctic region is characterised by its ocean. 
    The Arctic Ocean opens on both the North Atlantic and North Pacific. In 2025, it is still covered all year long by sea ice, but the ice pack extent decreases rapidly in area and thickness due to global warming, which has implications in energy feedback loops (see \cref{sec:polar_amp}). 
    In terms of radiation, the sea ice acts like a reflective shield. Therefore, the Arctic clouds have lesser cooling effect since they do not increase the albedo when flying over sea ice.
    The sea ice cover also inhibits the sea spray aerosol emissions of the Arctic ocean, which makes pristine the air of central Arctic. But mechanisms such as blowing snow\footnote{Blowing snow is the process of uplifted snow by wind, which could lead to the re-emission of ice crystals and aerosols particles into the atmosphere.}, or oceanic emissions through open leads\footnote{Open leads refer to cracks in the sea ice field which open on the ocean.} can dispute this assertion.
    On the other hand, open ocean region of the Arctic are known to be home of large plankton and phytoplankton populations, which can be a great source of biological aerosol particles.

    % Human activities
    Even though the Arctic is a remote region, it is far less isolated from human activities than the Antarctic continent. Indeed, because of geographical causes, almost \SI{90}{\percent} of the human population live in the boreal hemisphere.
    For the Arctic climate, it implies\----among other things\----an increased sensitivity to air pollution. Anthropogenic aerosols often reach Arctic latitudes, which can impact the radiative balance and have effect on the local cloud. 
    Once deposited, those particles can darken the snow and ice, contributing to lower the surface albedo.


%-----------------------------------------------
    \subsection{Polar amplification}
    \label{sec:polar_amp}
    The increase in average temperatures associated with global warming is not homogeneous across the globe. In some regions, warming is inhibited (for example, in the Southern Ocean, where the Antarctic Circumpolar Current benefits from the thermal inertia of deep ocean circulation to isolate Antarctica from warming for several decades), while in others it is amplified. As previously mentioned, the Arctic is the region where warming is most pronounced, with a rate of temperature increase about four times higher than the global average \citep{rantanen_arctic_2022}. Figure \ref{fig:arctic_warming} illustrates this meridional inhomogeneity of climate warming. 
This intense warming has striking consequences, for instance on the sea ice pack: the eighteen last years have seen the eighteen lowest sea ice extents since the beginning of observation 47 years ago.
    In this section, we review the main mechanisms that can explain this Arctic amplification.

        \begin{figure}[h]
            \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{./chap1/images/arctic_ampli.png}
            \caption{Annual mean surface temperature anomalies for the period 2004\--2024, relative to the 1951--1980 mean. The zonal mean anomalies are shown in the lower right panel. Warming reaches up to \SI{3}{\degreeCelsius} in the Arctic, approximately four times the magnitude observed at the equator. Data from \url{https://data.giss.nasa.gov/gistemp/maps/NASA GISS}}.
            \label{fig:arctic_warming}
        \end{figure}

    \subsubsection{Convergence of horizontal heat fluxes}
    Synoptic horizontal heat transport contributes to the amplification of warming at high northern latitudes. Although the list of relevant processes remains debated \citep{serreze_processes_2011}, the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation (AMO) appears to play a role in modulating Arctic temperatures. \citet{chylek_arctic_2009} showed that Arctic air temperature variations are linked to AMO phases. After at least sixty years of warming (1880–1940), the Arctic experienced a cooling period associated with a negative AMO phase. Following a loss of about 1°C over thirty years, temperatures began rising again when the AMO shifted back to a positive phase. However, establishing a robust correlation remains challenging, as AMO records only cover about one and a half oscillation cycles.
    
    The Arctic Oscillation (AO) also strongly influences the region’s climate \citep{quadrelli_simplified_2004}. The AO refers to a sea-level pressure (SLP) tripole at mid- and high northern latitudes (\cref{fig:SLP_AO}). A positive AO phase corresponds to a low-pressure anomaly centred over the Arctic, which strengthens the jet stream and supports the persistence of the polar vortex. In this phase, polar air remains confined to the Arctic and isolated from warm lower-latitude air. Conversely, a negative AO phase is characterized by an anticyclonic SLP anomaly over the Arctic, weakening the polar vortex. Cold air can then spill into the mid-latitudes, while the Arctic receives intrusions of warm air. Although Arctic warming follows multi-decadal cycles (e.g., AMO influence), it is systematically faster than the global mean. We can thus distinguish two categories of mechanisms:  
    (i) large-scale, multi-decadal drivers influencing the Arctic through climate cycles, and  
    (ii) seasonal to regional feedbacks specific to the Arctic.

        \begin{figure}[h]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{./chap1/images/SLP_AO.png}
            \caption{Climatology of sea level pressure (SLP) for the period 1979--2021, averaged over 50--90\degree N. On average, the Arctic exhibits a high-pressure anomaly, consistent with a negative phase of the Arctic Oscillation (AO). Data from \citet{copernicus_climate_change_service_era5_2017}.}
            \label{fig:SLP_AO}
        \end{figure}
    
    \subsubsection{Albedo feedback}
    Among its many specificities, the Arctic Ocean stands out from other basins due to the variability of its albedo. The presence of sea ice increases the basin’s albedo to 0.5–0.7 (50–70\% of incoming solar radiation reflected), compared to only 0.06 for open water.
    
    Sea ice thus acts as a reflective shield against solar radiation. Under climate warming, its extent tends to decrease year after year. The surface of open water\----i.e., not covered by ice\----increases, absorbing more energy (\cref{fig:aa_feedbacks} A). This temperature rises, directly affecting sea ice formation, making it thinner and less extensive. In parallel, as Arctic Ocean temperatures exceed air temperatures in winter, the excess sensible heat stored during summer is released back into the atmosphere in winter. This delayed effect of the sea ice–albedo feedback explains why temperature anomalies peak in winter \citep{serreze_processes_2011}.

        %\begin{figure}[h]
        %    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{./chap1/images/albedo_feedback.png}
        %    \caption{Schematics of the sea ice albedo feedback principle. First, a positive forcing induces an increase in temperature. This causes the melting of sea ice and snow, which leads to a decrease in albedo. Short wave radiation is then more strongly absorbed by the surface, which amplifies the temperature increase induced by the initial forcing, closing the positive feedback loop.}
        %    \label{fig:albedo_feedback}
        %\end{figure}
    
    A similar positive albedo feedback exists over Arctic land surfaces, but involves snow cover only (not sea ice). Snow has a very high albedo unless darkened by soot, ash, or other deposited particles (c.f. \cref{sec:deposition_processes}). Earlier snow melt\----triggered by positive climate forcing (e.g., enhanced greenhouse effect)\----reduces albedo and further accelerates melting.
    
    \citet{graversen_polar_2009} showed, by simulating Arctic amplification with fixed albedo, that albedo contributed about 15\% to the observed amplification. Other processes must therefore be considered to fully characterize Arctic amplification.
    
    \subsubsection{Lapse-rate feedback}
    Arctic temperature conditions and low solar exposure impose a strong atmospheric stability, often associated with a temperature inversion in the Arctic boundary layer. The maximum of the vertical temperature profile is not at the surface, but at an altitude ranging from a few hundred meters to more than one kilometre during the Siberian winter. The frequency, intensity, and height of these inversions vary strongly with season and location \citep{maillard_boundary_2022}. As a result, the excess heat from surface warming is not efficiently transported to higher atmospheric layers, but remains confined near the surface (\cref{fig:aa_feedbacks} B). Long wave radiation associated with this additional warming is also partly trapped in the lower atmosphere, contributing to positive feedback. \citet{pithan_arctic_2014} identified this mechanism as likely the main driver of Arctic amplification.

            \label{fig:aa_feedbacks}
        \end{figure}
    
    \subsubsection{Effect of polar clouds}
    Polar clouds are primarily low-level stratus \citep{shupe_clouds_2011}. During daytime, they play a dual role: reflecting incoming solar radiation and acting as a greenhouse “lid” for terrestrial infrared radiation. Observations by \citet{intrieri_annual_2002} show that the radiative balance of polar clouds is generally positive—warming the lower atmosphere—except for a short summer period when the surface albedo is minimal. On an annual average, polar clouds have a net warming effect on the surface (\cref{fig:warming_on_clouds}).
    While this cloud type tends to impose a net cooling at low and mid-latitudes, the Arctic warming effect is explained by the long polar night characteristic of high latitudes. During this period, cloud albedo has no impact on solar radiation, while their greenhouse effect persists and becomes the sole radiative forcing from clouds throughout the polar night.
    Furthermore, increasing temperatures lead to a predominance of the liquid phase over the ice phase in clouds. These liquid-rich clouds become optically thicker and have longer lifetimes. The resulting effect is warming, making this process another positive feedback.

\section{General intention and plan}

In this first chapter, we took a wide look at the state of the Earth's climate. By highlighting the evidences of its global warming, and by exploring the underlying causes, we brought to light the importance of clouds. 
The description of the cloud physics opened the topic of aerosol particles: these liquid or solid particles suspended in the air and that have the ability to craft clouds out of the blue, and to change their evolution pathways.
Finally, we explored the specificities of the Arctic climate which will be the background region of the studies presented in this thesis. \begin{center}
    \textbf{Is there causal relationships between aerosol particles sources, aerosol-cloud interactions and the rapidly warming Arctic climate, and do they constitute a feedback loop?}
    In other words, \textbf{how the aerosol particles populations responsible of the Arctic clouds formation will evolve in a warming climate?}

In order to address this general question, we will have to follow a broad investigation plan that tackle every links of this hypothetical feedback loop.
